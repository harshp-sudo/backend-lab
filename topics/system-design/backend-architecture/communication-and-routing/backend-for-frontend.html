Backend for Frontend (BFF)

🎯 The Hook & ELI5

ELI5 mental model. A museum offers one guide for young children and another for researchers. Both use the same exhibits, but they choose different detail, order, and pace. A Backend for Frontend gives a particular frontend experience a similarly tailored guide to backend capabilities, without making that guide the owner of every underlying fact.

A generic API can become awkward when mobile, desktop, and other experiences need substantially different data shapes or interaction flows. A Backend for Frontend, or BFF, is a backend boundary tailored to a frontend experience and often owned with that experience. It composes and translates domain capabilities so clients need less backend coordination.

WHY it matters: a delivery product’s courier app needs a compact next-stop view under variable connectivity, while its dispatcher console needs a wide operational overview. One response stuffed with every field can waste bandwidth and create shared release queues. Separate BFFs also have costs: additional code, runtime components, and the risk of duplicating business rules.

🗺️ High-Level Big Picture

Courier and dispatcher clients reach their respective experience backends. Both BFFs rely on Delivery, Customer, and Routing capabilities that remain authoritative for their own rules. A shared gateway can handle common entry concerns before routing to a BFF. The drawing shows two experiences because their needs differ; it is not a rule that every screen or device requires a separate backend.

🔍 Step-by-Step Mechanics

Follow the example one boundary at a time. Open each step to connect the familiar mental model to the internal mechanics.

1. Identify an experience with a real difference in needs

A children’s museum tour is different from a researcher’s visit because the questions and context differ, not merely because they use different shoes. Define the frontend experience: users’ goals, interaction flow, data volume, responsiveness, network conditions, and release ownership.

The courier app may need one upcoming delivery, minimal contact information, and a route summary. Dispatchers may need a filtered fleet overview and several operational actions. These are plausible reasons for tailored composition. Two clients that need nearly identical interactions may be served well by one shared API. Do not create one BFF per screen or per operating system by habit; choose a boundary whose ownership and behavior make sense over time.

2. Compose backend data into the experience contract

A guide selects relevant exhibits rather than making visitors find every curator themselves. The BFF calls the required capability contracts, combines their results, and returns a frontend-appropriate representation. That may reduce round trips, over-fetching, or client-side orchestration under constrained conditions.

For the courier’s next stop, Delivery owns the assignment and its state; Routing supplies an appropriate route result. The BFF decides how to present those facts for the courier flow. It should not silently redefine delivery completion or create a competing source of truth. Pagination, optional sections, image variants, and response size are experience decisions, while the underlying business invariants stay with their owners.

3. Keep workflow tailoring separate from domain rules

A guide chooses a route through the museum but cannot declare an artwork safe to touch against the curator’s rules. A BFF may coordinate the steps needed by a client interaction, but domain decisions such as whether a delivery can be reassigned must still be enforced by the authoritative capability.

If both courier and dispatcher BFFs independently implement assignment eligibility, the rules can diverge. Move shared business behavior into an owned domain capability rather than copying it or hiding it in an oversized shared frontend library. Some duplicate presentation mapping can be cheaper than forced coupling between experience teams. Distinguish duplication of harmless formatting from duplication of business authority.

4. Bound fan-out, latency, and partial failure

A tour guide must decide whether a closed optional exhibit should delay the entire visit. The BFF’s composite response similarly needs an explicit dependency policy. A missing decorative map preview may be tolerable; an unknown delivery assignment cannot be invented from an unrelated cached record.

Carry an overall request budget, use bounded concurrent calls where appropriate, and define optional fields/statuses honestly. Avoid retry multiplication between client, gateway, BFF, and services. Cache only with a clear key and freshness/authorization contract: a next-stop view for one courier or tenant must never become another caller’s response. A tailored backend can improve client latency while still overwhelming upstream services if its fan-out grows unchecked.

5. Align ownership and delivery with the experience

A guide cannot tailor the tour quickly if every sentence requires approval from a distant committee. A common reason for a BFF is to let a frontend-oriented team evolve its supporting contract without waiting for a universal API team’s backlog. The team needs ownership of deployment, monitoring, compatibility, and on-call responsibility as well as code.

Decide how supported client versions interact with BFF releases. Mobile clients may remain in the field for a long time, so backend changes must preserve supported contracts or provide a deliberate migration/version policy. Independent ownership does not mean the team can ignore domain service changes. Contract checks and cross-team communication are still required at those boundaries.

6. Keep the topology as simple as the experience allows

A museum does not need a separate guide for every doorway. Reassess whether each BFF earns its operational cost. A gateway plus two BFFs plus many services creates more hops and release artifacts than a shared application endpoint; that cost may or may not be justified.

A BFF need not mandate a microservices architecture. A tailored backend can compose capabilities within an existing application or consume a few coarse APIs. GraphQL can support flexible queries, but a query language alone does not settle ownership, authorization, domain authority, or client-specific workflows. Choose the smallest structure that supports the actual experience, and measure client request counts, payload size, user latency, and delivery coordination rather than celebrating a new backend component.

⚠️ Common Pitfalls, Bugs & What-If Scenarios

Treat a failure as a story: start with what a user observes, trace the boundary that failed, and choose a prevention that addresses the cause.

Two guides invent different business rules

Symptom: Courier and dispatcher actions disagree about whether reassignment is allowed.

Root Cause: Domain policy was duplicated inside experience-specific backends.

Fix/Prevention: Enforce the shared invariant at its domain owner; BFFs translate outcomes and tailor interactions rather than becoming competing authorities.

A faster client causes a slower system

Symptom: One next-stop page triggers dozens of backend calls and saturated services.

Root Cause: Reduced client round trips hid unbounded backend fan-out and retries.

Fix/Prevention: Measure complete call graphs, bound concurrency/deadlines, remove unnecessary dependencies, and design capability APIs suited to real usage.

Tailoring becomes a backend per screen

Symptom: Small interface changes require maintaining a growing collection of nearly identical servers.

Root Cause: The team used UI screen count instead of meaningful experience/ownership boundaries.

Fix/Prevention: Group coherent experience needs, keep shared capabilities authoritative, and retire boundaries whose cost exceeds their benefit.

🧠 Active Recall & Mastery Check

Summarize this in your own words

Explain the opening analogy, trace the worked example, and name one condition that would change your architectural decision. Try answering before opening the cards.

Does a separate mobile application always require its own BFF?

No. The decision depends on meaningful differences in interaction, payload, network constraints, ownership, and release cadence. If clients need essentially the same contract, a shared backend may be simpler. Device type alone is not a sufficient reason.

What should happen when two BFFs need the same delivery-eligibility decision?

The authoritative domain capability should enforce that rule so outcomes remain consistent. BFFs can tailor requests and presentation, but duplicating authority risks divergence. Shared presentation mapping and shared business invariants have different ownership trade-offs.

How is a BFF different from a thin shared gateway?

A thin gateway provides common entry routing and policies across clients. A BFF owns composition and interaction tailored to an experience. They can be combined, but adding both requires clear responsibilities so transformations, authentication, retries, and business decisions are not duplicated accidentally.

Does adopting GraphQL eliminate the architectural need to think about BFFs?

No. Flexible field selection may address some over-fetching, but experience ownership, workflow composition, authorization, query cost, failure policy, and domain authority still need decisions. A query API can be part of a BFF or a different design; the technology name does not settle the boundary.

Tricky & Most-Asked Interview Scenarios

Practice making a decision under a concrete production constraint. Open each answer, explain your reasoning aloud, then rate your confidence. The budgets and organizations below are author-created assumptions.

Mobile needs a tiny payload while desktop needs a rich dashboard. Should both keep one general API?

The Scenario & Constraint

A takeaway counter and a banquet hall need different serving arrangements from the same kitchen. Desktop requests rich reports, while mobile has limited bandwidth and needs a small combined summary. One general endpoint has growing conditionals and conflicting release demands. What boundary will you choose?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Introduce experience-specific backends when the difference is sustained enough to justify ownership: a mobile BFF can aggregate and shape a small response while the web BFF serves its richer composition. Keep authoritative pricing, permissions and inventory decisions in shared domain services, not copied into each experience backend.

Measure payload, round trips and team release friction before splitting. Give each BFF an owner, bounded downstream deadlines and contract tests with its frontend. It may sit behind a shared gateway that handles common edge concerns. Tailoring presentation does not require creating a new business-data authority.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

You gain client-focused contracts and more independent UI delivery, but maintain additional deployables, composition and compatibility checks. A single API with explicit projections is a viable alternative when differences are small and team coordination remains easy.

Follow-up trap: copying all backend logic into each BFF creates divergent business rules. If mobile and web converge on the same experience and ownership, reconsider whether two BFFs still pay their cost rather than retaining the split solely because the device labels differ.

iOS and Android need nearly identical data but have separate teams. One mobile BFF or two?

The Scenario & Constraint

Two counters serving the same meal may share a kitchen, unless independent menus keep colliding. iOS and Android currently need the same composition, but release separately and have different team ownership. A blanket one-BFF-per-device rule would create immediate duplication. What will you choose and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Start by evaluating experience and ownership coupling rather than counting device types. Use one mobile BFF if contracts and changes remain genuinely aligned with a clear ownership agreement. Split when incompatible release needs, composition or accountability repeatedly block one another, not merely because both platforms exist.

Track which changes require coordination and agree on compatibility support for old app versions. Shared business services remain common regardless of the BFF count. If teams choose separate BFFs, let each own its experience contract and avoid a shared runtime dependency that forces their releases back together.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

A shared BFF reduces duplicate operations and composition but requires coordinated governance. Separate BFFs provide autonomy at the cost of repeated presentation code and additional operations; both are legitimate under different change patterns.

Follow-up trap: two repositories backed by one mandatory shared-release library may retain lockstep coupling. If Android introduces an offline-specific synchronization model that iOS does not need, reevaluate the boundary. If only branding differs, separate deployments may offer little benefit.

Two BFFs duplicate aggregation code. Should you force both onto one shared library?

The Scenario & Constraint

Sharing a recipe helps until every restaurant must change its menu whenever the recipe changes. Web and mobile BFFs duplicate a little aggregation, and a proposed common library would require coordinated upgrades for every screen change. What will you share?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Separate stable technical helpers from experience-specific composition and authoritative business rules. Share low-volatility utilities only when their versioning and ownership do not erase independent releases. Keep changing screen orchestration local, accepting modest duplication where its meanings may diverge.

Move genuine business policy to its domain owner behind a contract rather than copying it into a common BFF library. Track dependency upgrades and compare the cost of duplication with the cost of coordination. Extract a library after stable common behavior emerges, not just because two code blocks initially look similar.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

You preserve frontend autonomy but may maintain some duplicated presentation code. A versioned shared library is a viable alternative for truly stable common functionality with independent adoption, and a shared service can fit a cohesive runtime capability with a clear availability budget.

Follow-up trap: a shared service for every helper adds network coupling without creating useful ownership. If duplicated code controls financial eligibility, it is not harmless presentation duplication; centralize the authoritative rule and keep both BFFs as consumers.

Web and mobile calculate different discounts. Which backend is authoritative?

The Scenario & Constraint

Two waiters should not invent different prices for the same order. Web and mobile BFFs independently calculate customer discounts, and releases have diverged. The business needs one auditable decision while screens remain different. What will you change?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Move discount eligibility and calculation to the pricing/order domain owner, with explicit inputs and an authoritative result. Both BFFs request that decision and shape its presentation without reimplementing the rule. Reconcile existing divergent behavior using agreed business examples and preserve quote/version identity where a later purchase must match an earlier price.

Add contract/integration checks for both BFFs and ensure object/tenant authorization remains enforced. Display-only formatting can stay local; rounding that changes the payable amount belongs in the authoritative financial decision, not in a device-specific formatter.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

You gain consistent policy and auditability, but depend on a shared domain capability and must budget its latency/availability. A locally cached quote can be a viable alternative when its version, expiry and acceptance semantics are explicitly supported.

Follow-up trap: returning a raw discount percentage and letting each BFF reconstruct the final amount may retain divergence. If regional rules genuinely differ, pass the relevant jurisdiction/context to the authority and model that difference explicitly rather than treating one BFF's divergent implementation as accidental customization.

A mobile app version must keep working for six months. How will the BFF evolve safely?

The Scenario & Constraint

A printed menu cannot update as quickly as the kitchen software. An installed mobile app may remain active for six months, while backend services change weekly. The BFF must support the old app contract without freezing the whole backend. What will you implement?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Treat the BFF as a compatibility boundary: evolve its public contract additively where safe and translate changing domain-service contracts behind it. Keep meaningful old behavior during a documented support window, with versioned endpoints or negotiated capabilities when semantics cannot remain compatible. Test representative supported app contracts against current composition.

Measure active versions and infrequent usage before retirement. Keep authorization and critical business rules current at the domain owner; old client support does not justify accepting an insecure operation. Communicate minimum-version and sunset policy explicitly, including what a user sees when support ends.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

You decouple domain releases from installed-client timing, but maintain adapters, old contract tests and support windows. A forced client upgrade is a viable alternative for a tightly managed internal app where availability and deployment policy permit it.

Follow-up trap: schema compatibility does not preserve behavior if a default or unit changes. If an urgent security issue makes an old operation unsafe, revoke or narrow that capability and provide a controlled upgrade/error path rather than promising unlimited backward compatibility at the expense of security.

One simple frontend already has a suitable API. Does adding a BFF solve a real problem?

The Scenario & Constraint

A small shop may not need a receptionist between the customer and the only counter. A single frontend uses two stable backend operations with acceptable latency and payload. A proposed BFF would forward both unchanged and introduce another deployment. What will you recommend?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Keep the simpler path until there is a concrete experience or ownership problem for a BFF to solve. Measure round trips, payload mismatch, security responsibilities and release friction. A reverse proxy or shared gateway may already handle the required edge behavior without another application composition service.

Keep frontend/backend contracts clear and tests representative so later evolution remains possible. If future screens require substantial composition or a second experience creates conflicting demands, introduce the BFF at that evidence-backed boundary. Avoid turning the absence of a BFF into permission for frontend code to own business invariants.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

You avoid an extra network hop and operational owner, while accepting that the client may coordinate the current small set of calls. A BFF is a viable alternative when server-side token handling, significant aggregation or frontend-team autonomy provides a concrete benefit.

Follow-up trap: a new security requirement may justify a server-side boundary even with one UI. If credentials or trust policy now require that component, reevaluate the decision and design its responsibilities explicitly. The right answer follows actual constraints, not a mandatory count of architectural boxes.

Real-world impact

For courier and dispatcher experiences, a BFF can make backend interaction fit the actual task and support independent frontend delivery. The benefit is a simpler experience contract with an accountable owner. Keep domain rules authoritative, control fan-out, and avoid creating a separate backend merely because another screen exists.
