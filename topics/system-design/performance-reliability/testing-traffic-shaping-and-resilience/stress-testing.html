Stress Testing

🎯 The Hook & ELI5

A lifeguard does not learn a pool’s emergency behavior by watching three calm swimmers. In a controlled rehearsal, the team increases demand, watches what becomes unsafe, stops at a planned boundary and checks that the pool returns to normal. Finding the breaking point without checking recovery is only half the rehearsal.

Stress testing investigates behavior above normal or intended capacity: where resources saturate, how queues and errors grow, which guarantees survive and how service recovers. It complements expected-load testing. A spike asks about abrupt change; a soak asks about sustained time-dependent effects. The exact taxonomy varies by tool, so state the experiment rather than relying on its label.

Our hypothetical isolated orders service normally meets objectives at 200 useful operations/s. We design a ramp beyond that level and a return to normal. Grafana k6 test-type guidance and original Google SRE failure analysis are references; no real production stress traffic or benchmark is executed by this lesson or dashboard.

🗺️ High-Level Big Picture

The test ends with evidence about recovery, not merely the moment a chart turns red. Stop criteria and isolation keep the experiment bounded; they are part of its design.

🔍 Step-by-Step Mechanics

Follow the worked example one decision at a time. Open each step to connect the everyday model to the implementation; examples state their product/runtime scope explicitly. Trace resource ownership, waiting, useful work and the recovery boundary.

1. State the failure question and containment boundary

Ask a precise question: at what offered load does useful throughput stop increasing, which queue/resource saturates first, and can critical reads survive while exports are rejected? Define the environment, dependencies, account/data isolation and stop owner. Use an authorized controlled environment rather than an arbitrary internet target.

First establish correctness and normal-load behavior. A broken basic path cannot explain a high-load failure. List stop conditions such as sustained objective failure, unsafe memory/storage growth or unintended dependency impact, and define how to stop generation even if the application becomes unresponsive.

2. Increase pressure slowly enough to interpret it

An original illustrative ramp uses 200, 250, 300 and 350 offered operations/s, holding each stage long enough to observe the intended phenomenon. Keep operation mix and generator evidence stable. Record delivered arrivals, useful completions, failures and retries, not just a configured target.

A short abrupt jump tests a different question from a gradual ramp. For autoscaling, include startup/cache-warmup and downstream-capacity effects. Do not infer permanent capacity from a momentary burst carried by preexisting spare resources.

3. Find the saturation knee and backlog mechanism

Suppose useful completion plateaus at 250/s while offered work reaches 350/s. Without rejection or reduced admission, a simplified queue accumulates 100 operations/s: 6,000 after one minute. If each pending task retains 16 KiB, that is about 93.75 MiB before overhead, even when throughput looks stable.

Inspect CPU/throttling, pool acquisition, locks, storage/network, queue age and memory to locate the limiting mechanism. The first flat throughput line is evidence, not proof that CPU is the bottleneck. A generator ceiling can look similar and must be ruled out.

4. Evaluate truthful degradation and isolation

Check whether critical work retains its stated behavior, optional work is explicitly degraded/rejected, and slow dependencies consume bounded resources. Verify responses do not report successful effects that were discarded. Retry attempts must be visible and bounded, or the service can keep itself overloaded after external demand stops.

Separate failure of the intended objective from failure of containment. A service may correctly shed excess demand while preserving critical operations; that can be a successful resilience result even though some test requests receive errors. Report both accepted and rejected work.

5. Reduce pressure and measure the recovery path

Return offered demand below sustainable useful capacity or stop it, then watch old queue age, retries, connection owners, memory and error rates. In a simplified example with 6,000 queued jobs, 250/s capacity and 200/s new work, only 50/s drains backlog, so ideal recovery takes 120 seconds before variation.

If queued work is already expired, executing all of it can prolong harm; cancellation/expiry policies and operation outcomes matter. Recovery may require safe operational intervention, but record that fact instead of claiming automatic recovery because a restart cleared the chart.

6. Choose a soak or spike follow-up when the evidence calls for it

If memory grows slowly at a sustainable rate, run a controlled longer soak to investigate lifecycle/resource accumulation. If deployment or a sudden event triggers failure, a spike experiment should exercise arrival rate-of-change, cold starts and retry synchronization. Keep explicit duration, budget and stop rules.

Record the reproducible workload, observed knee, dominant resource/failure chain, preserved/degraded guarantees and time to restored normal objectives. Change one mechanism and rerun the relevant experiment; a higher peak that leaves a much longer recovery tail may be a worse design.

⚠️ Common Pitfalls, Bugs & What-If Scenarios

Read each failure as a story: diagnose the visible symptom, identify its cause, and change the mechanism that produced it.

The highest accepted rate is called capacity

Symptom: Queues grow silently while the system returns initial acceptance.

Root Cause: Admission is mistaken for useful completed work.

Fix/Prevention: Measure completions, queue age, correctness and resource slope across a sustained window.

The test stops at first error

Symptom: No one knows whether the service recovers when demand falls.

Root Cause: Recovery was excluded from the experiment.

Fix/Prevention: Reserve a controlled ramp-down/observation phase with explicit normal-objective checks.

A stress label substitutes for a plan

Symptom: The generator is pointed at an uncontrolled shared dependency.

Root Cause: Workload, containment and stop ownership were never defined.

Fix/Prevention: Use an authorized environment with budgets, telemetry and a dependable stop path.

🧠 Active Recall & Mastery Check

Summarize this in your own words: Find the saturation mechanism in a controlled experiment, preserve truthful bounded behavior and prove how the system returns to normal after pressure falls. Explain a concrete example, name an assumption that changes the answer, and sketch the relevant diagram without looking.

What does stress testing add to expected-load testing?

It deliberately examines above-normal capacity, degradation, containment and return to useful normal behavior.

What happens at 350 arrivals/s and 250 completions/s without shedding?

The simplified backlog grows by 100 operations/s; accepted rate does not equal sustainable completion rate.

Why can recovery be slow after demand falls?

New demand, retries and old backlog still share finite completion capacity, so little capacity may remain to drain work.

When is a soak more informative than a short peak?

When the suspected failure depends on accumulation or long lifetimes, such as memory/resource leaks or storage growth.

Tricky & Most-Asked Interview Scenarios

Throughput plateaus at 250/s while arrivals reach 350/s. Is stability proven?

The Scenario & Constraint

The service accepts every request into an in-memory queue, useful completions stay flat and latency grows. What will you calculate and change?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Track the 100/s backlog growth and queue age/memory, locate the actual constrained resource and enforce bounded admission/deadlines. Preserve critical work and explicitly reject/defer excess demand rather than reporting indefinite acceptance. Re-run the recovery phase after the change.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

A larger queue may smooth a short burst but cannot sustain a permanent positive growth slope. Added execution capacity helps only if the bottleneck can use it. Report rejected work separately from failed accepted work so the resilience trade-off stays visible.

External traffic stops but the service remains overloaded. What keeps it busy?

The Scenario & Constraint

Timed-out clients and internal layers retry while old queued work continues. What evidence and recovery response will you use?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Separate original demand, retry attempts and old backlog; inspect attempt budgets, deadlines and useful completions. Bound retries with jitter and a single intentional retry owner, shed expired/unserviceable work safely, and observe recovery at reduced demand before restoring admission.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Dropping work requires truthful outcomes and idempotency/reconciliation for ambiguous writes. Restarting can clear local queues while losing accepted work, which is not automatic graceful recovery. More retries can perpetuate the failure they were meant to repair.

Six thousand queued jobs remain when normal 200/s traffic resumes. How long to recover?

The Scenario & Constraint

The measured simplified service capacity is 250 completions/s and jobs are equivalent; no retries or failures are assumed. What estimate and caveats will you present?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Only 50/s is available for backlog after new demand, so 6,000/50 = 120 seconds in this original idealized model. Verify actual queue age, job cost variation, deadline expiry and restored user objectives rather than treating the estimate as a timer guarantee.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Pausing new noncritical admission can drain faster at availability cost. Prioritizing only new work can starve old accepted jobs. If job cost or recovery capacity changes, the arithmetic must be recomputed from the new conditions.

A short spike fails despite an autoscaling maximum that looks sufficient. What do you test?

The Scenario & Constraint

New instances take time to start and warm caches, and they share one database. What experiment will distinguish steady capacity from burst response?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Use a bounded spike scenario with measured startup/warmup, delivered arrival schedule, queue/deadline behavior and downstream sessions/load. Compare prewarmed capacity, controlled admission and scaling changes, and include the post-spike recovery observation.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

A high replica maximum does not remove scale-up delay or database limits. Prewarming costs idle resources; long queues may violate callers’ deadlines. A gradual ramp alone may never exercise the sudden-change failure.

An hour of normal demand passes, but a day fails. Increase the peak instead?

The Scenario & Constraint

Memory and open-resource counts rise slowly at otherwise sustainable throughput. What test type and diagnosis fit?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Design a controlled soak long enough to observe the suspected lifetime, with resource slopes, settled baselines and normal/error/cancellation paths. Inspect ownership/retention instead of merely increasing peak arrival rate. Keep stop thresholds and cleanup/recovery evidence.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

A long test costs time and can suffer environment drift; use shorter targeted reproductions alongside it. A short stress peak can reveal a different limit but does not rule out time-dependent accumulation. Raising resource limits can postpone the symptom without fixing the leak.

Exports receive errors under stress while critical reads stay within their objective. Did resilience fail?

The Scenario & Constraint

The system intentionally sheds exports at saturation, exposes that outcome and preserves critical reads. What result will you report?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Report useful read behavior, export rejection rates, admitted-work correctness and recovery against the stated workload/priority contract. Treat intentional bounded shedding as the expected policy if those criteria are met, while documenting the capacity limit and degraded functionality.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Success-only latency can hide excessive shedding, so include offered and rejected demand. If exports had already been durably accepted, silently discarding them violates a different contract. Stress acceptance depends on explicit guarantees, not an unconditional zero-error slogan.
