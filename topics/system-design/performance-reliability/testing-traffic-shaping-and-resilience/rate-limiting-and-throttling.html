Rate Limiting & Throttling

🎯 The Hook & ELI5

A train station controls how many people enter a platform, spaces a crowd into safe groups and closes an entrance when the platform is full. Printing more waiting tickets does not make the platform larger. Under a surge, the useful decision is who may enter, who can wait briefly and who must receive an honest refusal.

This lesson builds on the existing API Rate Limiting lesson. Here the focus is operational traffic shaping, load shedding and backpressure during extreme surges, not another catalog of limiter algorithms. Rate limits constrain arrivals over time; concurrency limits constrain work in flight; shaping deliberately delays eligible work; shedding refuses work the service cannot usefully handle.

Our hypothetical service has a 300 ms interactive objective and shares resources with optional exports. Google SRE overload guidance informs the operational model, with HTTP response semantics from IETF references. All capacity and queue numbers are illustrative; the right policy depends on cost, priority, fairness and observable downstream behavior.

🗺️ High-Level Big Picture

The feedback loop includes both caller behavior and scarce execution resources. A per-identity quota alone cannot protect against a sudden increase in total identities or in per-request cost.

🔍 Step-by-Step Mechanics

Follow the worked example one decision at a time. Open each step to connect the everyday model to the implementation; examples state their product/runtime scope explicitly. Trace resource ownership, waiting, useful work and the recovery boundary.

1. Name the resource and useful-work objective

Choose the protected path: interactive reads must remain useful while exports may be delayed or rejected before acceptance. Measure operation cost and downstream waits, not only global requests/s. Ten cheap lookups and ten large exports impose different work.

Apply identity policy after trustworthy authentication/authorization context is available, while also using cheap infrastructure limits where necessary to protect that authentication path. Do not trust a user-supplied premium-priority flag or tenant string to allocate privileged capacity.

2. Compose arrival and concurrency budgets

A token or window policy can bound a caller’s arrival rate, while an in-flight permit bounds simultaneous work. An illustrative 1,000 arrivals/s with 200 ms mean occupancy implies roughly 200 active operations under stable conditions; if a dependency slows occupancy to two seconds, the same arrival rate implies roughly 2,000.

Rate compliance therefore does not guarantee resource safety. Bound in-flight work and queued demand around the measured downstream budget, and ensure every success, exception and cancellation releases its permit. Define how multiple service replicas share or approximate the policy.

3. Delay only when waiting still serves the contract

Shaping can smooth a short burst by releasing work gradually, but each delayed item consumes a queue slot, memory and deadline. If a service completes 100/s and the caller has only 200 ms for waiting, a backlog of 500 cannot be justified by calling it a burst buffer.

Use explicit count/byte/age bounds and recheck remaining deadlines at admission. Durable deferred business work needs a different acceptance/progress contract from an interactive request silently parked in RAM. A rate limiter is not itself a durable queue.

4. Shed by an explicit fair and criticality policy

Reserve bounded capacity for critical operations where justified, cap optional/bulk work, and avoid one noisy tenant monopolizing every queue. Fairness can be weighted, but total reserved/promised work must still fit shared resources. Check whether separate queues share one worker pool or database that defeats the intended reservation.

Reject before expensive optional parsing/processing where the protocol and security boundary allow. Degradation must preserve truth: omit a clearly optional section rather than fabricate an authoritative success. Already accepted durable work cannot be silently dropped as if it never entered.

5. Send feedback that reduces amplification

Use a response consistent with the API contract: 429 describes too many requests under a rate policy; 503 can describe temporary service unavailability/overload. Relevant responses may include Retry-After when a meaningful delay is available. That header is guidance, not a guarantee of future capacity or an instruction to retry non-idempotent writes blindly.

Bound retries, choose an intentional retry owner, use jitter and account for remaining deadline/idempotency. At extreme volume, even rejection handling can exhaust resources. Earlier edge controls and cooperative client throttling may be necessary to stop rejected work from consuming the origin.

6. Observe recovery and tune without oscillation

Track offered, admitted, delayed, rejected and completed work separately by bounded operation/priority classes, plus queue age, in-flight occupancy, waits and user outcomes. Avoid hiding failure by graphing latency only for a shrinking set of successful responses.

In a controlled surge experiment, verify that shedding stabilizes critical work, callers reduce retry pressure and capacity reopens gradually when saturation falls. A rapidly changing threshold can make queues and clients oscillate. Tune conservative budgets from evidence rather than presenting adaptive control as an automatic cure.

⚠️ Common Pitfalls, Bugs & What-If Scenarios

Read each failure as a story: diagnose the visible symptom, identify its cause, and change the mechanism that produced it.

Every tenant is within quota, yet the service collapses

Symptom: Aggregate in-flight work and queue age surge.

Root Cause: More tenants, costlier requests or slower dependencies invalidate a rate-only capacity model.

Fix/Prevention: Combine trusted fair usage with current concurrency/downstream budgets.

Throttling becomes an infinite waiting room

Symptom: Memory grows and callers time out before work starts.

Root Cause: Delay is unbounded or ignores the remaining deadline.

Fix/Prevention: Bound bytes/count/age and refuse work that cannot meet its objective.

Clients turn one error into many attempts

Symptom: Traffic stays high after the original surge subsides.

Root Cause: Multiple layers retry without shared limits or jitter.

Fix/Prevention: Choose bounded retry ownership, respect operation outcomes and propagate backpressure.

🧠 Active Recall & Mastery Check

Summarize this in your own words: Protect useful work by combining trusted fairness, bounded admission and waiting, honest refusal and feedback that reduces retry pressure through recovery. Explain a concrete example, name an assumption that changes the answer, and sketch the relevant diagram without looking.

Why does a fixed arrival limit not bound active work?

Active occupancy also depends on duration; slower work can accumulate many more simultaneous owners at the same rate.

How do shaping and shedding differ?

Shaping delays eligible work within a bounded contract; shedding refuses or explicitly degrades work to preserve useful service.

Does Retry-After guarantee a safe successful retry?

No. Future capacity and operation idempotency/outcome are separate concerns, and clients still need deadlines and bounded attempts.

Why graph rejected demand with latency?

Good latency for a tiny surviving fraction can hide severe unavailability; offered, admitted and completed work establish context.

Tricky & Most-Asked Interview Scenarios

Every caller meets its rate quota, but a slow database exhausts in-flight work. What changes?

The Scenario & Constraint

The request rate is steady while database hold time increases tenfold. The origin runs out of connections and memory. What will you do?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Add or tighten a bounded in-flight/downstream admission budget with explicit queue/deadline limits and fair policy. Investigate the database slowdown, shorten avoidable ownership and shed unserviceable optional work before queues grow without bound.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

A per-caller rate limit still serves fairness but is not current capacity. Increasing the pool can amplify contention. If admitted writes have unknown outcomes, error handling/retries must preserve operation identity rather than treat all refused and timed-out work identically.

A surge of exports starves checkout reads. How do you protect the critical path?

The Scenario & Constraint

Optional exports and interactive reads share workers and database sessions. Product policy allows refusing new exports during overload. What will you choose?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Use validated operation/tenant priority with bounded independent admission or reserved critical capacity, and reject/defer new exports under an explicit contract. Verify their shared worker/database/memory boundaries do not bypass the reservation. Observe read objectives and truthful export outcomes.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Reservations can strand idle capacity; weighted sharing is an alternative with more control complexity. Already durably accepted exports require retained ownership, not silent loss. A client-supplied priority header is not authority to consume critical capacity.

Can a five-second shaping queue serve a 300 ms interactive request?

The Scenario & Constraint

The system delays excess work for seconds and calls it throttling. Callers time out and retry before execution starts. What policy fits?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Bound waiting by the remaining interactive deadline and queue resource budget, then refuse excess work early with a truthful response. If the product permits later completion, use a separate durable acceptance/progress design rather than a hidden RAM queue.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

A queue can smooth short bursts when useful completion remains possible, but long waiting can create duplicate attempts and wasted work. Merely returning a longer client timeout changes the contract and may worsen resource retention.

The service spends most CPU returning errors. Why does shedding fail to stabilize it?

The Scenario & Constraint

Origin traffic is extreme; even authentication and constructing rejection responses consume its capacity. What additional controls will you use?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Move suitable cheap protection earlier at the edge/connection boundary, keep rejection paths minimal and bounded, and use cooperative client feedback/adaptive throttling where available. Keep security checks and fair policy at the correct trusted boundary; observe whether offered origin work actually falls.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Origin-side rejection is not free. Earlier controls may have coarser identity or fairness and can reject legitimate traffic. Avoid bypassing authentication on privileged paths just to save CPU; choose layered budgets with explicit limitations.

Three layers each allow four attempts during overload. What is the trap?

The Scenario & Constraint

A caller, gateway and service each retry a failed downstream operation independently. One request can cause a multiplicative attempt tree. What will you change?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Choose an intentional retry owner, bound attempts and total retry demand, propagate remaining deadlines and use jitter. Separate known-rejected work from ambiguous writes and use idempotency/reconciliation where effects may exist. Observe attempts per useful business completion.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Four attempts at three layers can produce up to 64 deepest-layer attempts in a simplified worst case. Retry-After is useful guidance but not an idempotency guarantee. Removing every retry may reduce resilience to a transient fault; retain only justified bounded recovery.

Each of twenty replicas enforces a local 100/s limit. Is the global API capped at 100/s?

The Scenario & Constraint

The policy is intended as an aggregate protection boundary, but requests spread across replicas and scaling changes the total. What will you decide?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

State whether the budget is per-instance or shared, and implement/document an aggregate mechanism or deliberate allocation/approximation across replicas. Verify behavior during scale-out, uneven routing and limiter-state failure, alongside the separate resource concurrency cap.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

A coordinated limiter adds latency/availability and state consistency trade-offs; local coarse protection remains useful even with a global policy. Do not advertise an exact global bound from independent local counters or assume a managed gateway makes every quota exact.
