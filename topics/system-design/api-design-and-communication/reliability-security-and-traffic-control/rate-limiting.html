Rate Limiting

🎯 The Hook & ELI5

A playground gives each child a small bucket of entry tokens. Tokens refill over time: a full bucket permits a short burst, but a child cannot keep entering faster than tokens refill forever. The playground also has a separate limit on how many children can be inside at once. APIs likewise need both fair arrival limits and protection for work already running.

Rate limiting controls admitted work over time for a chosen identity or resource. A quota may cover a longer accounting interval; a concurrency limit bounds simultaneous in-flight work. These are related but different controls. Count what costs capacity: one cheap lookup and one expensive export need not consume identical units.

Our example protects a paid tenant API while allowing a small interactive burst. Algorithm examples assume equally weighted requests unless stated otherwise. Product references include ASP.NET Core 10 algorithm distinctions and Amazon API Gateway REST usage-plan limitations; neither local middleware nor a managed feature label automatically supplies an exact global financial cap.

🗺️ High-Level Big Picture

A rate decision concerns arrivals under a policy; it does not authorize object access or ensure the dependency has an available execution slot. Compose those boundaries explicitly.

🔍 Step-by-Step Mechanics

Follow the worked example one decision at a time. Open each step to connect the everyday model to the implementation; protocol and implementation examples state their scope explicitly. Trace the same client operation through success, uncertainty and recovery.

1. Define the policy before choosing a counter

Identify the protected resource, identity and interval: tenant plus endpoint class, authenticated user, or another trusted dimension. An IP-only policy can unfairly group an office behind NAT and may be bypassed through many addresses. User-supplied tenant headers are not trusted quota identity.

Specify whether a limit is exact or permits bounded overshoot, what happens during policy-store failure and whether rejected work is queued or immediately refused. Bound key cardinality/expiry so attacker-created keys cannot turn the limiter into the memory bottleneck.

2. Choose the algorithm by burst and precision needs

A fixed window is simple, but two bursts on either side of its boundary can arrive close together. Sliding logs track individual arrivals more precisely at higher storage cost; approximate sliding counters reduce state with an explicit approximation. Token buckets separate burst capacity from sustained refill. Leaky-bucket shaping smooths departures, usually by queueing or controlled service, which has latency costs.

For a token bucket of capacity 100 and refill 10 tokens/second, an initially full bucket permits an initial burst of 100, then sustained admission near 10/second when requests each cost one token. Over an interval t starting full, admitted work is bounded by capacity plus refill times t under the stated model. Clock/refill arithmetic and weighted cost must be implemented consistently.

3. Make a distributed decision coherent

A read-then-write counter in several processes can admit multiple requests against the same last token. Use an atomic policy operation within the chosen storage/ownership boundary, with coherent refill and expiration. Test concurrent arrivals, clock assumptions and failover behavior.

One independent bucket per application instance multiplies the available budget as the fleet grows. Choose central coordination, key ownership, or leased/local sub-budgets with documented overshoot and recovery. Exact global coordination adds latency and availability trade-offs; do not hide them behind an in-memory counter example.

4. Tell callers when to wait and stop retry amplification

For an HTTP rate refusal, 429 can explain the policy and may include Retry-After. It is optional under the referenced RFC; a server should offer useful truthful guidance where it can. This example uses a documented short wait, not a universal reset rule.

HTTP/1.1 429 Too Many Requests
Content-Type: application/json
Retry-After: 2
Cache-Control: no-store

{"code":"tenant_rate_limited","retryAfterSeconds":2}

Clients should honor the policy within a bounded total retry budget and avoid synchronized retries. A rate refusal is different from invalid credentials or an unknown mutation outcome. Do not automatically repeat a side-effecting operation under a fresh identity merely because a later attempt might be admitted.

5. Protect work duration and observe fairness

Even a modest arrival rate can overload a slow dependency if operations remain active for a long time. Add bounded concurrency, deadlines and bounded queueing where necessary; release permits on completion/cancellation. A rate budget does not fix an indefinitely hanging worker.

Observe per-policy admitted/denied counts, key cardinality, latency, queue depth, store failures and overshoot. Load-test fleet scaling and outage recovery. A hard payment/spend invariant should be enforced by an authoritative accounting decision, not assumed from best-effort throttling. Give different workload classes appropriate capacity isolation.

⚠️ Common Pitfalls, Bugs & What-If Scenarios

Read each failure as a story: diagnose the visible symptom, identify its cause, and change the mechanism that produced it.

Scaling doubles a tenant’s quota

Symptom: Adding instances admits more traffic.

Root Cause: Each process holds a full supposedly global bucket.

Fix/Prevention: Coordinate ownership or allocate bounded sub-budgets with explicit overshoot.

The last token is spent twice

Symptom: Concurrent requests exceed the policy unexpectedly.

Root Cause: Counter/refill checks use separate non-atomic operations.

Fix/Prevention: Use one atomic policy decision in its actual storage boundary.

A rate-limited service still collapses

Symptom: Active requests grow while arrivals stay under limit.

Root Cause: Work duration increased and no concurrency/timeout bound exists.

Fix/Prevention: Bound in-flight work and queueing as separate capacity controls.

🧠 Active Recall & Mastery Check

Summarize this in your own words: A useful limit names the identity, cost, burst, time window, distribution accuracy and failure policy; protect in-flight capacity separately from request arrival rate. Explain a concrete example, name an assumption that changes the answer, and sketch the relevant diagram without looking.

Which token-bucket setting controls bursts?

Capacity bounds stored tokens and the initial burst; refill controls sustained admission. Both matter, as do request weights and global versus local ownership.

Can a concurrency limiter replace a per-minute rate limit?

No. It bounds simultaneous work, not total arrivals over time. Fast operations can pass many requests through a small concurrency limit.

Why is a separate read/check/write unsafe?

Concurrent requests may all observe the same remaining budget and spend it. The admission update must be atomic within the chosen policy boundary.

Must every 429 include Retry-After?

The cited HTTP extension permits it but does not require it. Document truthful retry behavior and distinguish a rate refusal from other failures.

Tricky & Most-Asked Interview Scenarios

A tenant gets four times its quota after scaling to four pods. What was mis-scoped?

The Scenario & Constraint

The product promises one tenant-wide budget, but every instance initializes a full bucket. What will you change?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Move the decision to a coherent tenant policy owner or atomic shared store, or allocate explicit local sub-budgets with a documented total/overshoot bound. Test adding/removing instances and failover. Keep policy identity based on authenticated tenant context.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Shared decisions add network and failure dependency; approximate local allocation can be faster if bounded overshoot is acceptable. A static divide-by-pod-count can break during rolling deploys and uneven routing. State clearly whether the product needs fairness or an exact accounting invariant.

A 100-per-minute limit admits 200 almost back to back. Is the counter necessarily wrong?

The Scenario & Constraint

One hundred requests arrive just before the fixed window ends and another hundred just after it starts. A dependency cannot absorb that burst. What will you do?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Explain that the fixed-window policy permits this boundary behavior, then choose a burst-aware token bucket, sliding policy or explicit concurrency gate according to the dependency’s needs. Load-test boundary arrivals and document the allowed burst separately from long-term rate.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

More accurate sliding state costs storage/coordination; reducing a fixed limit is a coarse alternative but may unnecessarily penalize normal traffic. A token bucket also intentionally allows its configured burst, so choose capacity deliberately instead of assuming every algorithm smooths traffic equally.

The limiter’s shared store is unavailable. Fail open or fail closed?

The Scenario & Constraint

Interactive catalog reads can tolerate bounded abuse, while an expensive export endpoint could exhaust capacity. The same unconditional failure policy serves both. What will you choose?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Define failure policy by endpoint risk: use a conservative bounded local fallback for acceptable reads if justified, and refuse or tightly gate expensive work when safe capacity cannot be established. Alert on fallback and document overshoot. Keep authentication and domain authorization enforced regardless of limiter availability.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Failing closed reduces availability; failing open exposes capacity/cost risk. Preallocated local budgets can bound some outages but require expiry/reconciliation rules. Best-effort gateway quotas are not a hard financial cap, so strict spend limits need an authoritative business control.

An office is blocked by an IP limit while one attacker rotates addresses. What changes?

The Scenario & Constraint

Hundreds of legitimate users share one public IP. Authenticated API tenants have separate paid budgets. How will you choose keys?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Use trusted authenticated tenant/user dimensions for their policy, with appropriate additional network abuse controls. Separate anonymous admission from authenticated quotas and bound key state. Analyze fairness and endpoint cost rather than treating every source address as one person.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

More identities increase state/cardinality and may enable account-based abuse; layered coarse IP controls remain useful for other threats. Never trust a caller-provided tenant header without authentication. A token valid for quota identity still does not authorize access to every object.

Requests meet their rate limit but still exhaust database connections. Why?

The Scenario & Constraint

Arrival rate is unchanged, but a dependency slows from 50 ms to five seconds. In-flight work climbs. What will you add?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Bound concurrency and queue length for that dependency, enforce deadlines/cancellation and isolate workload classes. Inspect resource wait times and recovery behavior. Keep rate policy for sustained admission, but protect active occupancy separately and reject or defer work explicitly when capacity is unavailable.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Concurrency caps can lower throughput and increase refusals; asynchronous jobs may fit delay-tolerant work. Merely raising the per-minute quota or connection pool worsens pressure. A queue without a bound only converts overload into latency and memory growth.

Every rejected client retries at the same second. How do you prevent another spike?

The Scenario & Constraint

A 429 response causes thousands of clients to retry immediately or at the identical reset boundary. The service oscillates between overload and rejection. What will you do?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Publish truthful retry guidance where known, use bounded client retry budgets with appropriate jitter, and avoid huge synchronized admission releases. Distinguish normal rate rejection from server overload and monitor attempts per logical operation. Preserve duplicate identity for any repeated side-effecting intent.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Jitter increases some callers’ wait; paced server admission or a bounded queue may suit controlled jobs better. Retry-After is not a reservation of future capacity. Clients must still cap total attempts and not assume a timed-out earlier mutation was rejected.
