TTL & Eviction Policies

🎯 The Hook & ELI5

A fridge discards milk after its use-by time, while a crowded shelf may remove a perfectly fresh box to make space. Those are different decisions. Cache TTL ends time-based reuse; eviction selects what to remove when capacity is constrained. A long lifetime cannot create unlimited shelf space or make an old source suddenly fresh.

TTL means time to live under the chosen engine/API contract. Expiry, capacity eviction, explicit invalidation and process loss can all remove an entry for different reasons. Define which event affects freshness, which affects residency and what a miss does to the origin.

Our examples compare Redis 8.0-compatible core expiry/eviction concepts, the Memcached 1.6 basic text lifetime encoding and general policy families. Provider/runtime versions and settings matter; not every policy in a conceptual matrix is implemented by every product. Example durations and capacities are teaching choices, not benchmark recommendations.

🗺️ High-Level Big Picture

Passing the TTL check is only one part of a usable hit. An entry can disappear earlier for capacity or lifecycle reasons, and source freshness still needs a separate argument.

🔍 Step-by-Step Mechanics

Follow the worked example one decision at a time. Open each step to connect the everyday model to the implementation; examples state their product/runtime scope explicitly. Trace the copy, authoritative owner, acknowledgment and recovery boundary.

1. Start with freshness and ownership, not a default number

Ask how old a displayed value may be, which changes require prompt invalidation and whether the next action validates against authority. A thirty-second public description and an access revocation may have incompatible policies even if both fit in one cache.

Choose fixed-from-write versus sliding/idle behavior explicitly. Repeatedly extending lifetime on reads can keep a popular stale entry alive under an idle-expiry design. Check engine semantics rather than assuming every “TTL” resets on hit or expires relative to the same clock event.

2. Account for source age and layered lifetimes

A cache populated from a stale replica can start a new TTL on an already old view. Resetting an application timestamp does not reset the age of the fact. If the requirement is a maximum source-age bound, establish trustworthy source/version/age assumptions and account for every tier; otherwise describe a weaker bounded-reuse policy honestly.

HTTP caches have defined age/freshness/validation rules, while script-managed caches and data stores can behave differently. Do not naively add fresh max-age at each intermediary as permission to extend a policy beyond the intended bound. An invalidation/version check or authoritative bypass may be necessary for strict reads.

3. Choose a capacity policy against the workload

Eviction policies optimize retained usefulness under pressure; they are not consistency models. Measure actual product behavior, including approximation, metadata and eligible key subsets.

In Redis, verify maxmemory-policy and whether keys are eligible: volatile policies rely on expiry-bearing keys. Separate durable state from indiscriminately evictable views. Memcached’s actual item allocation and eviction diagnostics need their own review.

4. Use jitter and negative lifetimes within the real budget

Identical fill times and lifetimes can synchronize expiry. Spread refresh/expiry where useful, but keep the maximum within the actual freshness promise. If a freshly read view can be reused for at most sixty seconds, choosing forty-five to sixty spreads expiry without deliberately exceeding that ceiling; it still does not solve unknown source staleness.

Use a separate short negative policy for confirmed absence where delayed creation visibility is acceptable. Jitter does not prevent many simultaneous readers of one already missing key from all loading it. Combine with coalescing/admission where that failure matters.

5. Measure memory, expiry and miss recovery together

Budget payload, keys, metadata, fragmentation/runtime overhead and aggregate copies across workers/tier replicas. One hundred thousand values averaging two KiB already need about 195 MiB of payload, before those additions. maxmemory is not a direct RSS ceiling for every operational buffer.

Observe expired/evicted keys, admission failures, usable-hit rate, miss cost and origin work. Test a scan-heavy workload, hot-set changes, near-capacity operation and synchronized cold start. Verify expiry encoding: the Memcached basic protocol treats values above thirty days as timestamps, while zero does not promise residency forever.

⚠️ Common Pitfalls, Bugs & What-If Scenarios

Read each failure as a story: diagnose the visible symptom, identify its cause, and change the mechanism that produced it.

TTL is described as guaranteed residency

Symptom: An unexpired value disappears.

Root Cause: Capacity eviction or server lifecycle was ignored.

Fix/Prevention: Handle misses regardless of age and test the selected eviction policy.

A new TTL blesses old source data

Symptom: Views remain older than promised.

Root Cause: A lagging source/tier repeatedly starts fresh lifetimes.

Fix/Prevention: Account for source/version age and use a stronger read path where needed.

Jitter violates the ceiling

Symptom: Some values live longer than the agreed bound.

Root Cause: Only positive random time was added to a maximum lifetime.

Fix/Prevention: Choose a bounded distribution consistent with the full freshness model.

🧠 Active Recall & Mastery Check

Summarize this in your own words: Use TTL for an explicit reuse/freshness policy and eviction for a measured capacity policy; account for source age, jitter limits and the miss traffic every removal creates. Explain a concrete example, name an assumption that changes the answer, and sketch the relevant diagram without looking.

Why can a key vanish before its TTL?

Eviction, explicit deletion or process/server loss can remove it independently of time-based expiration.

Does a newly assigned TTL prove a newly current fact?

No. The value may come from an old source snapshot. Lifetime and source-age correctness are different arguments.

Does TTL jitter fix a single hot missing key?

Not by itself. It spreads entries’ expiry times, but concurrent readers of one miss still need coalescing or bounded origin admission.

What should guide eviction choice?

Measured reuse/skew, value size/cost, adaptation needs, product eligibility/implementation and the miss load the origin can handle.

Tricky & Most-Asked Interview Scenarios

Keys with an hour left on TTL disappear under load. Is expiration broken?

The Scenario & Constraint

The cache reaches its memory budget during a scan-heavy workload. What will you inspect?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Separate expiry from capacity eviction, inspect actual policy/eligibility and key-size/reuse metrics, and measure which removals create expensive misses. Bound admission or choose a better-fitting policy/working set rather than assuming TTL reserves space.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

More memory may help a real working-set limit; selective caching can be cheaper. No-eviction can trade removals for write errors. An hour of lifetime is not a promise that the entry survives memory pressure or restart.

A ten-second cache serves minute-old values. How is that possible?

The Scenario & Constraint

A lagging replica supplies old data on every fill, each with a new TTL. What will you change?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Define required source freshness/version and fill from a suitable authoritative or minimum-version path where needed. Account for upstream age and prevent indefinite re-blessing of old data. Measure replica/fill age independently from cache lifetime.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Fresh-source reads cost capacity/latency; a weaker stale-display contract may be acceptable. A short TTL controls reuse after fill, not automatically age relative to the authoritative change.

A hot key never refreshes because reads renew its idle lifetime. What policy fits?

The Scenario & Constraint

The product expects periodic freshness even for continuously accessed data. What will you choose?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Use an appropriate fixed freshness deadline or independent validation/refresh policy rather than relying only on idle expiry. Retain the actual data/source version and test sustained hot traffic. Verify how the selected cache API resets expiration on reads/writes.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Fixed expiry can create repeated hot-key regeneration, so coalescing/stale policy may be needed. Idle expiry is useful for reclaiming inactive entries under a different goal; it does not by itself guarantee periodic source refresh.

A sixty-second ceiling becomes ninety seconds after jitter. What is wrong?

The Scenario & Constraint

Engineers add zero-to-thirty seconds to every configured sixty-second lifetime. The full freshness contract matters. What will you do?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Choose a distribution whose upper bound fits the justified reuse budget, account for source/tier age and keep stricter changes on an appropriate invalidation/read path. Test maximum, not only average, policy behavior. Add coalescing for same-key misses separately.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Earlier expiry increases origin reads; scheduling proactive refresh can be another controlled choice. Jitter cannot make an unknown-age source fresh and does not independently fence a late stale fill.

A nightly scan evicts hot interactive data. Which policy change is justified?

The Scenario & Constraint

Recency-based retention follows the scan while expensive frequently reused keys disappear. What will you measure and compare?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Measure reuse/size/miss cost by workload and compare admission filtering, isolation or a suitable frequency-aware policy supported by the engine. Test adaptation after hot-set changes and include origin load, not just hit counts.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Frequency history can retain yesterday’s hot items too long; separate pools or bypassing low-value scan caching may be simpler. No policy universally wins, and an engine’s approximation/eligibility can differ from a textbook algorithm.

Missing-item caching reduces load but delays new product visibility. What is the trade-off?

The Scenario & Constraint

Bots request nonexistent IDs repeatedly while real users create products. What will you choose?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Use a scoped short negative lifetime only for confirmed absence, invalidate/version creation as required and bound aggregate origin admission. Keep errors distinct from absent records and test late negative fills after creation.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Shorter negatives increase repeated origin work; a trustworthy existence/version service may help at additional cost. Longer TTLs save reads but cannot be called immediately consistent. Never negative-cache a timeout as proof the object is absent.
