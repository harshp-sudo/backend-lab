Caching Fundamentals

🎯 The Hook & ELI5

Keep the book you use every day on your desk instead of walking to the library every time. You save trips, but your desk is small and a copied timetable may become outdated. A cache is that nearby reusable copy: its value comes from avoiding repeated work while keeping the answer acceptable.

Caching stores reusable results closer to their consumers or in a cheaper access path. The origin is the authoritative source for the example, while cache entries can be rebuilt. A hit finds a usable entry; a miss needs another path. Finding a key is not enough if its representation, permission scope or freshness is wrong.

Our worked example serves a public product description. The product database owns facts, readers tolerate thirty seconds of ordinary staleness, and price confirmation at purchase still uses the authoritative operation. These are teaching assumptions, not a universal policy for every product field. Cache decisions begin with workload and correctness, before choosing Redis or another technology.

🗺️ High-Level Big Picture

Treat usability as identity, representation and freshness together. The origin path must work under a stated capacity budget; the cache is not evidence that an answer is authoritative.

🔍 Step-by-Step Mechanics

Follow the worked example one decision at a time. Open each step to connect the everyday model to the implementation; examples state their product/runtime scope explicitly. Trace the copy, authoritative owner, acknowledgment and recovery boundary.

1. Choose repeatable work and a source of truth

Measure an expensive repeated read or computation, identify its inputs and decide which component owns the answer. Public descriptions, rendered fragments and derived aggregates can have different lifetimes. A value that changes on every request may gain little from storage while adding lookup and invalidation costs.

State what can be discarded and rebuilt. If a cache entry is the only record of a payment deduplication decision or accepted deferred write, losing it can change business correctness. That state has become part of an authoritative promise and needs a different durability/recovery design.

2. Make the key represent the reusable result

Include meaningful input dimensions such as item identity, tenant, locale, representation version and visibility policy. Keep keys canonical and bounded. Two requests can share a value only if their result contracts permit reuse; matching a URL or object ID alone may not be enough.

Choose the cacheable unit deliberately. A whole dashboard can save more composition work but depends on many changing facts. A smaller object has narrower invalidation scope but may require several lookups. Account for serialization, network transfer and response assembly rather than comparing storage engines in isolation.

3. Use a simple load model, then measure tails

Suppose the service receives 1,000 reads/second and 95% are usable hits. Under a one-origin-read-per-miss model, normal miss traffic is 50 reads/second. A cold cache could send 1,000, so an origin sized for 150 needs admission and recovery control.

A cost model, not a production benchmark

This average is not a p95/p99 prediction. Trace misses, large values, queueing and skew independently. A tiny hit-rate improvement on cheap data can be less valuable than removing a few very expensive misses.

4. Specify freshness, absence and refresh

Choose whether an entry expires by age, is invalidated on changes, or carries a version that can be checked. A TTL is a reuse/lifetime policy, not proof that the origin read used to fill it was current. Keep correctness-sensitive operations on an appropriate authoritative path.

Distinguish confirmed absence from a failed origin lookup. A short negative entry can reduce repeated requests for a nonexistent item, but caching a timeout as “not found” hides real data. Define creation/deletion and permission-change behavior as well as ordinary updates.

5. Design cache loss as a normal operating mode

Bound origin concurrency and retry traffic, coalesce overlapping work where applicable and warm high-value entries gradually. Decide which reads may use an explicitly allowed stale copy, which must fail and which can be deferred. A universal fail-open bypass can move the outage into the database.

Observe usable-hit rate, miss reasons, origin load, fill latency, error rate, bytes and eviction/expiry. Test restart, partial cache failure, key distribution change and an empty fleet. Revisit capacity when traffic or origin performance changes; yesterday’s safe cache can become today’s critical dependency.

⚠️ Common Pitfalls, Bugs & What-If Scenarios

Read each failure as a story: diagnose the visible symptom, identify its cause, and change the mechanism that produced it.

A high hit rate hides expensive misses

Symptom: The dashboard is slow despite 99% hits.

Root Cause: The remaining misses are huge or queue behind hot work.

Fix/Prevention: Measure cost-weighted misses and latency tails, not hit ratio alone.

A timeout becomes a missing product

Symptom: Existing products disappear temporarily.

Root Cause: Origin errors were cached as confirmed absence.

Fix/Prevention: Use distinct result/error states and negative-cache only verified absence under policy.

A restart takes down the database

Symptom: All clients bypass the empty cache together.

Root Cause: Origin capacity assumed normal hit behavior.

Fix/Prevention: Bound fallback admission and warm useful keys deliberately.

🧠 Active Recall & Mastery Check

Summarize this in your own words: Cache repeatable work under an explicit reuse and freshness contract, measure its actual value and keep the origin protected when the useful copies disappear. Explain a concrete example, name an assumption that changes the answer, and sketch the relevant diagram without looking.

Why is “key found” weaker than a usable hit?

The value may belong to another scope, be too old or use the wrong representation. Reuse must satisfy identity, permission and freshness policy.

How many normal origin reads does the 1,000 RPS, 95% hit model predict?

Fifty per second under the stated one-read-per-miss assumption. Cold-start traffic can be much larger, and queues/retries can amplify it.

When is “we can lose the cache” unsafe?

When it contains the only record needed for an accepted business effect or another durable guarantee. Such state needs explicit authoritative recovery.

Can average cache latency establish p99?

No. Miss costs, size, queueing and failures shape the distribution; measure the actual tail paths separately.

Tricky & Most-Asked Interview Scenarios

Hit rate is 99%, but p99 is still bad. What do you inspect?

The Scenario & Constraint

A product endpoint reports excellent hits while a few requests take seconds. The team wants more cache memory. What will you do?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Trace slow requests by key class, result size, cache lookup, origin wait and fill work. Measure cost-weighted misses and queueing, then fix the actual expensive path or choose a better cacheable unit. Compare before/after under the same request mix.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

More memory may help capacity misses, but targeted access/query improvements can be better. An average or aggregate hit ratio hides rare costly work. If slow requests are cache hits transferring giant values, changing TTL will not remove serialization/network cost.

The cache is empty and the origin cannot absorb full traffic. What is the recovery plan?

The Scenario & Constraint

Normal traffic is 1,000 RPS with 50 origin reads/second. A restart removes the cache and origin capacity is 150 reads/second. What will you do?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Admit bounded origin work, coalesce overlapping keys, prioritize useful warm-up and apply explicit degradation/refusal for excess demand. Use stale copies only where retained and policy permits. Measure recovery rather than opening unrestricted fallback.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Some users wait or receive reduced service; overprovisioning origin for full uncached load is another cost choice. Coalescing one hot key does not reduce distinct cold-key work. Unbounded retries can prevent recovery altogether.

A product lookup times out. Should you cache “not found”?

The Scenario & Constraint

Repeated requests are expensive, but a transient origin failure cannot establish whether the item exists. What result do you store?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Keep failure distinct from authoritative absence, return an honest bounded error/degraded result and control repeated origin work separately. Negative-cache only a confirmed absent result under a documented short lifetime and creation/invalidation policy.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Negative caching can protect the origin but delays visibility of newly created data; direct authoritative checks may suit correctness-sensitive reads. Treating every exception as absence converts an availability fault into incorrect product state.

Two users ask for the same item ID but see different fields. Can the key be only the ID?

The Scenario & Constraint

A shared cache stores a representation with customer-specific prices and permissions. Isolation is mandatory. What will you choose?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Bypass unsafe shared reuse or define a reviewed identity/visibility-aware key and authorization policy. Separate a truly public description from private fields where that simplifies reuse. Test cross-user, cross-tenant and permission-change requests through real hits.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

More key variants reduce reuse and increase invalidation work; origin reads are simpler if affordable. A unique object ID is not permission. TTL only limits exposure duration and does not make an unauthorized representation acceptable.

The “disposable cache” stores the only payment deduplication record. What must change?

The Scenario & Constraint

Eviction permits an old payment request to execute again. The service had assumed every Redis key could be lost safely. What will you do?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Move the durable operation identity/outcome into an authoritative boundary or explicitly engineer storage/recovery to uphold the business promise. Keep a rebuildable lookup copy only as an optimization. Reconcile uncertain effects before permitting a new execution.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Durable state adds retention and coordination; a naturally idempotent domain operation may simplify some cases. Giving a key a long TTL does not protect it from every eviction, failure or inconsistent recovery. Cache product choice alone cannot establish exactly-once effects.

Nearly every request has unique parameters. Is adding a cache still a win?

The Scenario & Constraint

A report endpoint generates one-off personalized queries. Cache lookup/serialization adds latency and memory fills with entries never read again. What will you do?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Measure reuse and avoid or restrict caching for that workload. Consider caching stable shared sub-results or precomputing a bounded useful view only when ownership/freshness is clear. Apply admission based on actual value rather than store every result.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Selective admission costs policy and measurement; keeping the direct origin path is a valid simpler choice. A larger cache cannot manufacture temporal reuse. If the workload later develops hot repeated keys, reevaluate with real evidence.
