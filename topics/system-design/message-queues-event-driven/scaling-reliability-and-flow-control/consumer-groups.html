Consumer Groups

🎯 The Hook & ELI5

A newspaper office gives each delivery worker a neighborhood, so workers in that office share the route list. A different newspaper office has its own full list and progress. Hiring ten more workers does not help if every difficult delivery must happen on one narrow street in strict order. Changing the assignment also does not instantly stop a former worker already driving there.

A consumer group lets instances of one logical application share consumption work and progress under a broker’s membership/assignment protocol. Separate applications that each need every event require independent subscription/group state. Here the concrete model is Kafka 4.1, Java 4.1.2 and the classic group protocol; other protocols and products must be checked against their own contracts.

Suppose a fraud projection consumes account events from six partitions. Members divide those partitions and recover from committed positions. The design must handle membership churn, slow processing, skew, duplicate effects and stale owners—not just a cheerful diagram showing more workers. RabbitMQ competing queue consumers and SQS workers are useful comparisons, but are not interchangeable Kafka partition-assignment implementations.

🗺️ High-Level Big Picture

Within the chosen classic Kafka group, one partition is assigned to one member at a time by the protocol. Different groups maintain independent positions; assignment does not make external effects mutually exclusive forever.

🔍 Step-by-Step Mechanics

Follow the worked example one decision at a time. Open each step to connect the everyday model to the implementation; examples state their product/runtime scope explicitly. Trace the actual actor, communication, acknowledgment and recovery boundary.

1. Give each logical application the right identity

Fraud and audit need independent group IDs when each requires the eligible retained input. Giving them the same group ID can split work between them instead of providing each a complete view. Replicas of the same fraud application normally share that application’s group identity.

Define group ownership, topic permissions, reset policy and retention expectations. A fresh group’s starting position depends on explicit offsets and reset configuration; it does not automatically mean replay all history. Document whether bootstrapping starts at retained history, a snapshot handoff or future-only input.

2. Understand assignment and the parallelism ceiling

In this classic Kafka example, members are assigned partitions and only one member is the assigned consumer for a partition in that group at a given moment. With six partitions, adding a seventh member cannot create a seventh independent assigned partition lane. Other groups can read the same retained records independently.

Partition count and key distribution affect useful parallelism. Increasing partitions can change key mapping and ordering transitions, so treat it as a data-placement change. A single hot ordered key cannot be safely accelerated by splitting its dependent effects without revisiting the business invariant.

3. Manage membership and rebalances deliberately

Members join, leave or are deemed unavailable; the coordinator/assignment protocol reallocates work. Long processing, crashes and rolling deploys can trigger movement. Revocation/reassignment requires coordinating unfinished work and committed progress according to the actual client protocol.

Name the group protocol and client version before discussing timeout/heartbeat tuning. Kafka’s classic and newer consumer protocols have different configuration responsibility. This lesson deliberately scopes its examples to classic behavior rather than presenting one timeout recipe as universal. Tuning must follow observed processing and recovery budgets.

4. Keep polling, worker completion and commits consistent

The Java consumer has a documented threading contract. If application workers process records off the polling thread, synchronize completed-work tracking and commit only the contiguous completed prefix of each partition. Bound the pending work rather than allowing unlimited fetched records to accumulate.

When work is revoked, stop accepting new work for the revoked ownership, coordinate in-flight completion/cancellation and use supported commit handling. Do not let stale worker callbacks advance a new owner’s progress. A failed commit can reflect lost ownership and must not be treated as confirmation that effects and recovery position agree.

5. Protect external effects from obsolete workers

A partition reassignment does not terminate a slow remote call or database transaction already issued by the previous process. A new owner can process the same record while an old effect is still in flight. Stable effect identity and supported conditional/fencing mechanisms at the actual sink may be necessary.

A local lease expiration is also not proof that the previous worker stopped. Exactly-once Kafka output under a supported transaction has its own fencing/progress contract, but unrelated HTTP/SQL effects do not inherit it. Trace the real output boundary and choose idempotency, reconciliation or an explicit pending workflow.

6. Scale from lag shape and business age

Observe per-partition lag/age, processing latency, rebalance frequency, commit errors and completed business effects. High lag on all evenly loaded partitions can justify more workers up to useful parallelism or faster processing. One hot partition calls for key/work analysis rather than blind horizontal scaling.

A newly reset group can show a low lag number while having intentionally skipped history. A committed position can also precede unresolved external effects if the application acknowledged too early. Verify outcome metrics and bootstrap/reset events. This is a documented model and failure trace; no Kafka cluster or rebalance benchmark is run here.

⚠️ Common Pitfalls, Bugs & What-If Scenarios

Read each failure as a story: diagnose the visible symptom, identify its cause, and change the mechanism that produced it.

Audit misses fraud’s records

Symptom: Each application sees only part of the stream.

Root Cause: Distinct roles accidentally share one group.

Fix/Prevention: Use independent application groups and a verified bootstrap/recovery plan.

Extra consumers sit idle

Symptom: More instances do not reduce the hot backlog.

Root Cause: Assigned partitions or a hot serial key limit parallelism.

Fix/Prevention: Measure per-partition/key load before changing capacity or mapping.

Old worker writes after reassignment

Symptom: The same business effect races between two owners.

Root Cause: Protocol reassignment was treated as cancellation of external work.

Fix/Prevention: Use supported sink identity/fencing and coordinate progress/recovery.

🧠 Active Recall & Mastery Check

Summarize this in your own words: Treat consumer groups as owned progress and assignment protocols for one application role. Scale from partition/key reality, and protect effects across membership changes at the sink that actually commits them. Explain a concrete example, name an assumption that changes the answer, and sketch the relevant diagram without looking.

When should two services share a group identity?

When they are interchangeable instances of one logical consumption role, not when each independently needs every event.

What limits partition-assigned concurrency in the chosen model?

The number and distribution of assigned partitions, plus ordering and downstream capacity constraints.

What does reassignment not automatically stop?

External effects or tasks already running in an obsolete process.

Why is consumer lag alone insufficient?

It can hide skew, skipped/reset history or effects acknowledged before completion; business age and outcomes matter too.

Tricky & Most-Asked Interview Scenarios

Audit and fraud each see only half the events. What is misconfigured?

The Scenario & Constraint

Both independently require the complete stream but use one group ID. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Give the applications independent group identities and plan a retained-history/snapshot bootstrap to recover missed input without repeating unsafe effects.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Independent groups add load and progress state. Interchangeable replicas of the same role should still share work; do not create a group per worker accidentally.

Eight members consume six partitions and lag remains. Should you add more?

The Scenario & Constraint

Two members are unassigned and one partition dominates lag. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Inspect per-partition/key load and downstream latency, optimize the hot lane and change partitioning only if the invariant permits a safe migration.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

More members cannot invent partition lanes or split a strictly ordered hot key. A broader partition redesign can improve distribution but adds mapping/cutover obligations.

A long task repeatedly triggers reassignment. How do you stabilize it?

The Scenario & Constraint

The classic-protocol Java consumer cannot meet its configured poll/processing budget. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Measure task duration, bound fetched/in-flight work and use a supported polling/worker coordination design. Tune applicable timeouts only with an explicit recovery budget.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Longer timeouts can delay failure recovery; asynchronous work adds completion/commit complexity. Newer protocol settings are not assumed equivalent to this scoped classic example.

A worker pool finishes later offsets first. How do you commit safely?

The Scenario & Constraint

A crash must recover all unfinished records in each partition. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Track durable completion per partition and commit only beyond the contiguous completed prefix, coordinating revocation and limiting outstanding work.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

A slow earlier task can hold the checkpoint. Serial processing simplifies bookkeeping; committing the largest finished offset trades correctness for apparent progress.

The old owner calls a provider after rebalance. What protects the business?

The Scenario & Constraint

The replacement is already retrying the same intent. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Use stable provider-supported identity/outcome reconciliation or a supported fenced local effect, and prevent obsolete callbacks from advancing current progress.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

The sink must actually enforce the mechanism. Broker group ownership alone cannot revoke a remote HTTP request already accepted by a provider.

A fresh group shows zero lag but lacks old accounts. What happened?

The Scenario & Constraint

It began after the retained historical records it needed, or reset to a future position. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Inspect group initialization/reset configuration and retained boundaries, then bootstrap from available authoritative history/snapshot and coordinate live catch-up.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

History outside retention may be unrecoverable from Kafka. Low lag only measures distance from a chosen position; it does not establish complete business state.
