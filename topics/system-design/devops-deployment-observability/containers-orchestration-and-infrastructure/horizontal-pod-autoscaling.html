Horizontal Pod Autoscaling

🎯 The Hook & ELI5

A café manager periodically checks how busy each barista is and asks for more staff when they are overloaded. “Busy” depends on what one person was expected to handle. Sending a hiring request does not instantly put trained people behind the counter, and hiring more cashiers cannot fix a broken coffee machine.

Horizontal Pod Autoscaling, or HPA, adjusts a scalable Kubernetes workload’s desired replica count from observed metrics. It is a delayed feedback controller, not immediate serving capacity or node provisioning. Its behavior depends on metric type, resource requests, readiness, bounds and configured policies. Follow a CPU-based API workload, then consider why the same rule fails when work is blocked on a database or metrics disappear.

🗺️ High-Level Big Picture

The feedback loop contains measurement, reconciliation, scheduling and startup delays. Desired replicas are only one intermediate state.

🔍 Step-by-Step Mechanics

Follow the worked example one decision at a time. Open each step to connect the everyday model to the implementation; examples state their product/runtime scope explicitly. Trace ownership, state transitions, decision evidence and the failure/recovery boundary.

1. Choose a metric that tracks the capacity problem

CPU utilization is useful when added replicas can share CPU-bound work. Queue depth or another well-defined demand metric may fit workers, but metric units and per-Pod versus total semantics matter. For request-normalized CPU utilization, the relevant configured CPU requests supply the denominator. A metrics API and collector/adapter must actually provide fresh values; Metrics Server is a common separate add-on for resource metrics, not an automatic consequence of creating an HPA object.

2. Work the simplified ratio with explicit assumptions

With three ready equivalent replicas each requesting 500 millicores and using 400 millicores, utilization is 400/500=80%. At a 50% target, the basic recommendation is ceil(3 × 80/50)=5 replicas. This assumes complete suitable metrics and omits tolerance, missing/unready handling, bounds and configured scaling policies. The denominator is the request, not a CPU limit or the whole node. Changing requests can change the recommendation even when measured usage is unchanged.

3. Apply bounds and behavior without inventing capacity

minReplicas/maxReplicas constrain desired count; scale-up/down policies and stabilization govern how recommendations become changes. Use the autoscaling/v2 behavior supported by the cluster and metric adapter, and verify actual settings rather than presenting a remembered default as universal. Stabilization reduces oscillation at the cost of slower changes and possibly idle capacity. Node scaling is separate: a request for five Pods can leave two Pending if eligible resources are missing.

4. Interpret missing and startup metrics cautiously

Missing CPU requests can make utilization undefined for affected Pods; a missing metric is not a measured zero. Kubernetes uses conservative handling of missing/unready observations and multiple-metric failures in its algorithm. Inspect HPA conditions and reported metric availability before changing targets. Startup CPU spikes or unready replicas can distort evidence; meaningful startup/readiness design and appropriate controller configuration are part of the solution, not a reason to ignore all warmup behavior.

5. Bound dependency load and verify the loop

Suppose each replica allows 20 database connections and maxReplicas is 15: the application can attempt 300 connections before other clients and rollout overlap are counted. Verify that downstream systems can sustain it, then cap per-instance concurrency and admission as needed. During a surge, use queueing/load shedding and minimum capacity according to deadlines while new Pods start. Observe actual ready endpoints, latency and dependency saturation; scale-in must drain safely and preserve accepted work.

⚠️ Common Pitfalls, Bugs & What-If Scenarios

Read each failure as a story: diagnose the visible symptom, identify its cause, and change the mechanism that produced it.

Wrong denominator

Symptom: A request change unexpectedly doubles replicas.

Root Cause: CPU utilization is treated as fraction of node or limit.

Fix/Prevention: Calculate usage/request explicitly and re-evaluate autoscaling when requests change.

Missing treated as zero

Symptom: Autoscaling policy is trusted despite absent data.

Root Cause: Telemetry failure is mistaken for low demand.

Fix/Prevention: Inspect metric conditions/freshness and conservative fallback behavior.

Database avalanche

Symptom: More Pods worsen latency.

Root Cause: Replica growth multiplies downstream concurrency.

Fix/Prevention: Budget total connections/work, bound admission and address the actual bottleneck.

What basic count follows from 3 replicas at 80% versus a 50% target?

ceil(3 × 80/50)=5 under the simplified complete-metric assumptions. Real controller policies and bounds can alter the applied result.

What normalizes utilization-based CPU HPA?

Relevant configured CPU requests, not node CPU or limits. Missing required requests can make utilization undefined.

Why are five desired replicas not five serving replicas?

Scheduling, node availability, image pulls, initialization and readiness take time and may fail.

Why can successful scaling hurt the service?

Every new replica can add database connections, retries or other shared load. Scaling must fit downstream capacity and admission policy.

Tricky & Most-Asked Interview Scenarios

Three Pods use 400m against 500m requests at a 50% target. What is the initial recommendation?

The Scenario & Constraint

All three replicas are ready with complete equal CPU observations; ignore tolerance/stabilization and use a sufficient max bound. What will you calculate?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Compute 80% utilization from usage/request, then ceil(3 × 80/50)=5. Check applied behavior, actual ready count and downstream headroom rather than treating the arithmetic as instantaneous capacity.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Limits and node size are different quantities. If metrics, readiness, bounds or policy assumptions change, the real result can differ; a precise formula needs an equally precise scope.

CPU usage stays the same but replica demand rises after lowering requests. Why?

The Scenario & Constraint

Each Pod still uses 400m, but its request falls from 500m to 250m while the utilization target stays 50%. What explanation and response fit?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Recalculate the denominator: utilization rises from 80% to 160%, so the simplified recommendation from three is ceil(3 × 160/50)=10. Size requests and HPA targets together using measured behavior and feasible placement.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Artificially inflating requests can reduce reported utilization while consuming reserved scheduling capacity. A lower request is not an optimization if it breaks placement, fairness or the autoscaling model.

CPU utilization shows unknown after deployment. Should it be treated as idle?

The Scenario & Constraint

The metrics service has gaps and one container lacks a CPU request. Traffic and latency remain high. What will you inspect?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Check HPA conditions, relevant resource requests and metrics API availability/freshness, repair the measurement chain and use an explicit capacity/overload fallback. Do not convert unknown into zero.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Kubernetes applies algorithm-specific conservative behavior for missing data; it does not justify guessing a desired count from absent observations. Increasing maxReplicas alone cannot fix unavailable metrics.

HPA asks for twenty replicas but eight stay Pending. What action fits?

The Scenario & Constraint

The scaling recommendation is recorded, yet ready count and latency do not improve. What boundary do you diagnose?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Inspect scheduler events, eligible node capacity, quotas, affinity and storage constraints. Coordinate node capacity or policy changes and protect service with bounded admission while startup/placement catches up.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

HPA changes workload scale, not node supply. Extra desired replicas can remain unusable indefinitely, and reducing requests blindly may create runtime contention after placement.

Fifteen Pods each allow twenty database connections. Is scaling safe against a 200-connection server?

The Scenario & Constraint

Other database clients and rollout overlap also need capacity. What control will you add?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Recognize the application alone can attempt 300 connections, then derive a smaller total budget with reserves and bound per-instance pools/concurrency or effective replica exposure. Diagnose the shared database bottleneck and shed/queue work within deadlines.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

A larger max count can worsen starvation instead of adding throughput. Pools are often lazy, so 300 is a configured potential rather than measured current use; account for real demand as well as the bound.

Replica count swings every few minutes and drops accepted work. What changes?

The Scenario & Constraint

A bursty workload warms slowly and scales down before queued work drains. CPU-based recommendations fluctuate. What policy fits?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Use workload-appropriate metrics, minimum/headroom and configured scaling behavior/stabilization; validate readiness and graceful scale-in with accepted-work accounting. Measure loop delay and downstream limits before increasing aggression.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Longer stabilization costs idle capacity and does not fix a misleading metric. Queue length alone ignores job cost/age, and slow warmup means a reactive controller cannot guarantee immediate surge capacity.
