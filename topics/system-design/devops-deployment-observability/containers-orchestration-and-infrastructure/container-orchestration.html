Container Orchestration

🎯 The Hook & ELI5

Running one food stall is manageable with a checklist. Running fifty stalls needs a dispatcher: which site has room, who replaces an absent cook, and how do customers find an open stall? The dispatcher can move staff, but cannot invent ingredients, resolve double-booked kitchens or repair the accounting ledger by itself.

Container orchestration coordinates many container instances over a pool of machines. It turns declared intent into repeated placement, restart, discovery and replacement decisions. Kubernetes is one implementation; managed container services and simpler hosted runtimes offer different ownership and control. This lesson focuses on the platform decision and its failure boundaries, rather than repeating the Kubernetes component tour. Consider a team moving from a few API processes to multiple services across failure zones.

🗺️ High-Level Big Picture

A reconciler repeatedly narrows the gap between desired and observed state, subject to real capacity, policy and application constraints.

🔍 Step-by-Step Mechanics

Follow the worked example one decision at a time. Open each step to connect the everyday model to the implementation; examples state their product/runtime scope explicitly. Trace ownership, state transitions, decision evidence and the failure/recovery boundary.

1. List duties before choosing a product

Identify workload count, release frequency, failure tolerance, network/storage needs and staffing. Packaging, scheduling, service discovery, secrets delivery, telemetry and data recovery have different owners even if a platform integrates their APIs. A managed service may take over selected infrastructure operations, not application correctness or every incident. Compare the operational work removed with the new control plane and policies the team must learn.

2. Express a durable service contract

Declare the intended task count, resources, health checks, identity, configuration and replacement policy. A one-off script can launch three containers, but it does not continuously restore that count after machine failure. Reconciliation compares observed state and acts repeatedly, so operations must tolerate retries and partial progress. Avoid two independent systems fighting to own the same instances or desired count.

3. Place for capacity and failure domains

Suppose six equivalent workers are needed at peak and must survive loss of one of three zones. Even placement of three workers per zone gives nine total and leaves six after losing one zone, under this simplified equal-capacity model. Six workers evenly spread leave only four. This is arithmetic, not a universal availability design: storage, quotas, traffic, correlated dependencies and placement feasibility can still defeat the assumption.

4. Reconcile failure without multiplying harm

A missing heartbeat is evidence of uncertainty, not proof a process has stopped. Restarting elsewhere may overlap with a partitioned old instance. Stateless request workers often tolerate overlap; a single-writer database may need fencing and a data-system failover protocol. Configure meaningful health checks and bounded restarts so a shared dependency incident does not become a fleet-wide restart storm.

5. Budget the platform and practice exit paths

Account for upgrades, identity, networking, storage integrations, capacity, security policy, observability and support. Test node loss, unavailable control plane and replacement with persistent state. Keep a way to inspect underlying work when the management UI fails. Moving between orchestrators may require changes in identity, networking, storage and rollout semantics even when the image format is portable. Choose the least operational burden that still meets the actual contract.

⚠️ Common Pitfalls, Bugs & What-If Scenarios

Read each failure as a story: diagnose the visible symptom, identify its cause, and change the mechanism that produced it.

More replicas in one failure domain

Symptom: A zone outage removes every copy.

Root Cause: Count was optimized without placement constraints.

Fix/Prevention: Plan remaining capacity and enforce feasible spread across relevant domains.

Restart storm

Symptom: Every service restarts during a database outage.

Root Cause: Dependency failures trigger liveness reactions rather than controlled degraded service.

Fix/Prevention: Separate readiness, liveness and dependency policy; bound retries and restore the real bottleneck.

Platform adopted without owners

Symptom: Deployments work until a certificate or upgrade fails.

Root Cause: Initial installation was mistaken for complete lifecycle operation.

Fix/Prevention: Assign platform duties and rehearse maintenance/recovery before relying on the platform.

🧠 Active Recall & Mastery Check

Summarize this in your own words: Choose orchestration by the operational duties it must perform, then verify placement, surviving capacity and data ownership rather than equating task replacement with recovery. Explain a concrete example, name an assumption that changes the answer, and sketch the relevant diagram without looking.

How does orchestration differ from a launch script?

It continually compares intended and observed state and acts again after changes/failures, rather than ending after one launch sequence.

Why is replica count alone insufficient?

Copies can share a host, zone or dependency and fail together. Surviving capacity and feasible placement matter.

Why can rescheduling stateful work be dangerous?

The old process may still run during uncertainty. Data ownership/fencing must prevent incompatible concurrent writers.

Does a portable image make platforms interchangeable?

No. Identity, storage, networking, health, scaling and rollout semantics still differ and require explicit migration work.

Tricky & Most-Asked Interview Scenarios

Three services and two engineers: should you adopt a full cluster?

The Scenario & Constraint

The team needs simple deployments and has no dedicated platform operator. Traffic is modest. What decision process fits?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Compare a supervised host or managed runtime against concrete availability, storage and release requirements. Adopt a cluster only if its needed capabilities justify ongoing identity, network, upgrade and recovery duties.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Simpler hosting can impose scaling/isolation limits; more powerful orchestration can overwhelm the team’s operations budget. Popularity and future hypothetical scale are weaker evidence than a real unmet requirement.

Nine workers are spread 7/1/1 across zones. Can you promise six survive any zone loss?

The Scenario & Constraint

Six equal-capacity workers are required during peak and the largest zone can fail. What placement/capacity change fits?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Model remaining capacity for each zone loss and enforce feasible spread. In the simplified three-zone case, 3/3/3 leaves six after one zone fails; 7/1/1 leaves only two if the largest fails.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Extra replicas cost resources and still need database/network headroom. A spread rule cannot place into absent or full zones, and unequal worker capacities require a different model.

A node stops heartbeating but may still write. Is replacement alone safe?

The Scenario & Constraint

The workload owns an exclusive external job. Network isolation hides the old process from the controller. What will you protect?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Use an application/data ownership protocol with fencing or equivalent authority enforcement before allowing conflicting side effects. Treat rescheduling as process availability, separately from exclusive ownership correctness.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Waiting can reduce availability; immediate duplicate ownership can corrupt data. A scheduler’s belief that a node is unreachable is not proof the old process lost access to the shared dependency.

A database slowdown restarts the entire fleet. What policy is missing?

The Scenario & Constraint

Health checks classify every slow database call as a dead process and restart all API workers repeatedly. What changes?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Separate process liveness from serving readiness and dependency diagnosis, bound request/retry load and restore dependency capacity. Avoid a restart policy that destroys healthy local state and amplifies shared failure.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Removing all checks hides genuine hangs. Aggressive deep probes also load the dependency; choose bounded signals with distinct actions and validate degraded behavior.

A rescheduled worker starts with an empty local directory. Did orchestration lose the database?

The Scenario & Constraint

The service wrote durable state to a node-local path and the replacement landed on another machine. What contract must change?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Define durable storage and data recovery ownership explicitly, choose suitable volume/data replication semantics and verify replacement against real restore procedures. Keep disposable local state distinguishable.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Network storage adds cost and latency; replication can propagate bad writes. Task mobility does not imply local data mobility or a consistent backup.

A managed platform outage reveals nobody owns application backup restoration. Who should?

The Scenario & Constraint

The provider runs the control plane, but the team cannot recover its application data after deletion. What operational agreement fits?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Document provider versus team duties, assign application/data recovery ownership and test a complete restore with keys, configuration and dependencies. Select platform services against that end-to-end contract.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Delegating infrastructure removes selected work, not every failure responsibility. Buying replication alone may improve availability without preserving recoverable historical state.
