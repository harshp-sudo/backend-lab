Disaster Recovery & Backup Strategies

🎯 The Hook & ELI5

A spare notebook helps when the current one is lost, but only if its pages are readable, recent enough and stored away from the same fire. You also need the cabinet key and instructions for using it. A second notebook that instantly copies every mistake will faithfully copy an accidental deletion too.

Disaster recovery restores a usable business service after disruptive loss, corruption or unavailable infrastructure. Backups preserve recoverable historical state; replication and fault tolerance help continuity but solve different failures. Recovery includes data, keys, identity, configuration, dependencies, traffic and verification. Follow a PostgreSQL-backed order service recovering from an accidental deletion discovered after it replicated. All timings below are hypothetical planning arithmetic; no production restore or cloud failover was executed for this lesson.

🗺️ High-Level Big Picture

A backup file is one input. The outcome is verified business service at an explicitly accepted recovery point, with controlled ownership of future writes.

🔍 Step-by-Step Mechanics

Follow the worked example one decision at a time. Open each step to connect the everyday model to the implementation; examples state their product/runtime scope explicitly. Trace ownership, state transitions, decision evidence and the failure/recovery boundary.

1. Define recoverable business outcomes

Recovery Point Objective, RPO, limits acceptable data loss measured as a time gap to the recoverable point; Recovery Time Objective, RTO, sets the targeted service recovery duration under a defined incident clock. Specify the business operations and dependencies included. A report archive and payment ledger can need different objectives. Base a design on achievable tested procedures rather than selecting numbers from a storage product brochure.

2. Choose consistent backups and history

A consistent backup must fit the database/application protocol; copying arbitrary live files can yield unusable state. PostgreSQL 18 distinguishes logical dumps, filesystem-level backups and continuous archiving. Its PITR workflow requires a suitable base backup and continuous required WAL history, not a logical dump plus arbitrary WAL files. Missing a required segment can limit recovery. WAL also does not back up manually edited PostgreSQL configuration files, so retain those and other dependencies through appropriate separate procedures.

3. Protect availability of copies, keys and access

Store independent copies across relevant account/region/media/authority boundaries and retain enough history for delayed corruption discovery. Control deletion and verify immutable/retention behavior where used. S3 Object Lock protects object versions with different governance/compliance semantics; governance can be bypassed with required authority/request behavior, so it is not equivalent to an unchangeable policy for every administrator. Retained encrypted bytes are useless if keys or recovery identities are lost. Protect and rehearse access without granting every production operator the ability to destroy all copies.

4. Estimate the complete recovery path

Suppose 600 GB decimal must be restored at a sustained 100 MB/s decimal: transfer alone is 6,000 seconds, or 100 minutes. Add hypothetical 10 minutes detection, 20 provisioning and 15 verification/cutover for 145 minutes if all stages are serial. That cannot meet a 60-minute RTO under these assumptions. Compression, replay, startup, throttling and dependencies can change measured behavior; the arithmetic is a lower-level planning example, not a benchmark or guaranteed production time. For RPO, compare incident time with the latest actually recoverable point, including archive lag, not merely the schedule of the last backup job.

5. Restore in isolation, verify and return deliberately

Choose a clean point before corruption, restore to isolated infrastructure, verify database/application integrity and reconcile accepted external effects such as payments. Coordinate old/new writer authority and route updates so failover does not create two conflicting primaries. After recovery, failback needs controlled data synchronization, verification and another ownership transition. Run regular scoped restore drills that record achieved recovery point, timings, evidence and exceptions. A green backup job or an available replica is not proof of recoverability; actual environment restoration is required to substantiate that stronger claim.

⚠️ Common Pitfalls, Bugs & What-If Scenarios

Read each failure as a story: diagnose the visible symptom, identify its cause, and change the mechanism that produced it.

Replicated deletion

Symptom: Every live replica has the same missing orders.

Root Cause: Continuity copies propagate valid-looking destructive changes.

Fix/Prevention: Retain independent historical recovery points and verify corruption detection/restore.

Keys missing from the plan

Symptom: Encrypted backup bytes exist but cannot be opened.

Root Cause: Recovery identities, key availability or dependencies were omitted.

Fix/Prevention: Test access to the full recovery set under the target failure/authority boundary.

Backup success mistaken for RTO evidence

Symptom: Restoration takes far longer than promised.

Root Cause: Only creation of backup files was measured.

Fix/Prevention: Measure complete restore/verification/traffic recovery and revisit architecture or objectives.

How do RPO and RTO ask different questions?

RPO asks how far back acceptable recoverable data may be; RTO asks how long defined service recovery may take. State the incident clock and business scope.

Why does replication not replace historical backups?

It can propagate deletion or corruption. Recovering an earlier clean state requires retained history that survives the relevant failure/authority boundary.

What does PostgreSQL PITR need beyond a file called backup?

A suitable base backup and the continuous required WAL history, plus separately retained configuration/keys/dependencies and verified procedures.

What is transfer time for 600 GB at 100 MB/s in decimal units?

6,000 seconds or 100 minutes before provisioning, replay, verification or cutover. It is planning arithmetic, not measured restore performance.

Tricky & Most-Asked Interview Scenarios

Failure is at 12:00 but the latest verified recoverable point is 11:42. Does a 15-minute RPO hold?

The Scenario & Constraint

Backups were scheduled every ten minutes, but archiving/validation lagged. What evidence and response fit?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

The actual gap is 18 minutes, exceeding the stated 15-minute objective under these times. Identify the latest valid point, communicate/reconcile affected writes and fix the recovery-point pipeline rather than quoting the nominal schedule.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

A job start or uploaded file timestamp is not a verified recoverable point. Reducing the schedule alone may not fix archive gaps, and business loss reconciliation may involve external systems.

An accidental deletion reaches every replica. What recovery path fits?

The Scenario & Constraint

The error is discovered four hours later and the team has fast failover but no recent clean live copy. What will you do?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Contain further damage, select a retained clean historical recovery point and restore in isolation, then verify/reconcile later legitimate work before controlled return. Design retention to cover delayed discovery.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Promoting a current replica preserves the deletion. Returning to an earlier point can lose valid later changes; replay/reconciliation must distinguish bad and legitimate effects rather than blindly restoring everything.

A PostgreSQL base backup exists, but one required WAL segment is missing. Can you promise the target timestamp?

The Scenario & Constraint

Recovery requires a continuous archive through the chosen target. One segment in the necessary chain is unavailable. What response fits?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Determine the actual usable recovery boundary and locate a validated alternative backup/history chain if available. Report the achievable point and affected loss honestly, then repair archiving/verification controls.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

A newer filename elsewhere does not fill a missing chain segment. Logical dumps and filesystem base backups have different recovery workflows; do not treat arbitrary files as interchangeable PITR inputs.

An immutable encrypted backup survives, but its key service account was deleted. What is missing?

The Scenario & Constraint

The incident removes production identities and the only known decryption access. What recovery design fits?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Include supported key recovery/independent recovery identities, configuration and dependency access in the protected recovery set and test them under the planned failure boundary. Inspect whether the existing key material is recoverable before claiming the bytes can be restored.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Immutability protects selected deletion/overwrite behavior, not decryption ability or application integrity. Broadly copying secrets everywhere increases compromise risk; independent controlled access needs deliberate design.

Can a 600 GB restore at 100 MB/s meet a one-hour RTO?

The Scenario & Constraint

Assume decimal units, sustained transfer and serial 10-minute detection, 20-minute provisioning and 15-minute verification/cutover. What conclusion fits?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Transfer alone takes 100 minutes; the stated serial path totals 145 minutes. Choose measured improvements, pre-positioned/warm capacity or a revised objective and validate a complete drill rather than claiming the target is met.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Parallel stages, compression and faster storage can change timings but must be measured. A calculated throughput model is not an executed production restore or a guarantee under disaster contention.

The old region returns after failover. Can both accept writes during failback?

The Scenario & Constraint

The recovery region has accepted new orders while the original region retains an older view. What coordination fits?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Establish one intended write authority or a deliberately supported multiwriter conflict protocol, synchronize/validate data and plan a bounded cutover with reconciliation of accepted external effects. Verify the failback as carefully as failover.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Turning on old routing without ownership can produce split-brain and divergent orders. Active-active is a separate consistency design, not a switch that makes stale databases safe to merge automatically.
