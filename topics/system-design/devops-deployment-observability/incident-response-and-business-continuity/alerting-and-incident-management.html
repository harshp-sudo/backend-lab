Alerting & Incident Management

🎯 The Hook & ELI5

A smoke alarm is useful when it wakes someone who knows which room to check and what to do. Fifty alarms describing the same fire can make coordination harder. During the response, one person coordinates, others contain the fire, and someone keeps people informed. Finding the original faulty wire matters, but stopping harm comes first.

Alerting turns evidence into a timely request for human or automated action; incident management coordinates that action until the service and its risks are understood. Prometheus rules and Alertmanager provide a concrete notification pipeline, while Google SRE’s incident guidance informs roles and mitigation. Follow a checkout outage where a shared dependency generates many symptoms. Notification suppression, acknowledgement and service recovery are separate states.

🗺️ High-Level Big Picture

A firing condition, a delivered page, an acknowledged response and restored user outcomes are distinct boundaries with separate failure modes.

🔍 Step-by-Step Mechanics

Follow the worked example one decision at a time. Open each step to connect the everyday model to the implementation; examples state their product/runtime scope explicitly. Trace ownership, state transitions, decision evidence and the failure/recovery boundary.

1. Page for a decision and define urgency

Start from user impact, meaningful SLO burn or an imminent condition requiring timely intervention. Give the alert an owner, severity, evidence, runbook and an expected response. CPU at an arbitrary percentage may warrant investigation, not immediate wakeup. Keep diagnostic context available without turning every underlying metric into a page. Define who escalates if the assigned responder cannot acknowledge; urgency should reflect impact and time to act.

2. Separate rule evaluation from notification handling

Prometheus evaluates configured alert rules and sends active alerts to Alertmanager. Alertmanager handles grouping, deduplication, routing and notification suppression; it does not run the application or automatically repair it. A failed scrape or absent series needs explicit evaluation policy because many expressions simply stop matching when data disappears. Monitor rule evaluation and notification delivery through suitable independent checks; the monitoring system can fail at the moment it is needed.

3. Route and suppress with narrow scope

Alertmanager route trees use labels/matchers and inheritance. A matching child normally stops subsequent sibling matching unless continue is true. Group related symptoms by meaningful incident dimensions; grouping by every instance defeats consolidation. Inhibition suppresses selected notifications while a corresponding source alert fires; a silence is an explicitly scoped timed suppression. Neither proves the underlying problem is fixed. Validate matches, receiver ownership and expiry with safe local/test procedures rather than sending real incident notifications from a lesson.

4. Establish command, operations and communication

For a significant incident, name an incident commander to coordinate priorities and handoffs, an operations lead to organize mitigation and a communication lead to maintain consistent updates. Small teams may combine roles, but ownership must be explicit. Keep a shared timestamped timeline with hypotheses, evidence, changes, decisions and remaining risks. Avoid competing untracked changes; assign bounded investigations and reconvene results. Communicate known impact and next update time without inventing certainty about the cause or recovery deadline.

5. Mitigate, verify and learn

Prefer a safe containment/rollback or traffic action that reduces harm while diagnosis continues, respecting data compatibility and external effects. Verify recovery from user outcomes, backlog/data correctness and telemetry freshness, not only a quiet pager. Record handoff and follow-up ownership for residual risk. A blameless postmortem explains contributing conditions, what made response easier/harder and concrete prioritized actions with owners and validation; “be more careful” does not change the system that enabled failure.

⚠️ Common Pitfalls, Bugs & What-If Scenarios

Read each failure as a story: diagnose the visible symptom, identify its cause, and change the mechanism that produced it.

Suppression mistaken for resolution

Symptom: Incident is closed when pages stop.

Root Cause: A silence or route failure hides ongoing impact.

Fix/Prevention: Verify user outcomes and pipeline health; track suppression owner and expiry.

Everybody investigates, nobody coordinates

Symptom: Conflicting changes prolong recovery.

Root Cause: No explicit decision/communication ownership.

Fix/Prevention: Assign roles and track bounded hypotheses and changes in a shared timeline.

Missing measurements look healthy

Symptom: A broken collector removes the alert series.

Root Cause: Absent data was treated as successful service.

Fix/Prevention: Define missing/freshness checks and independent coverage where needed.

🧠 Active Recall & Mastery Check

Summarize this in your own words: Connect an actionable symptom to the right owner, coordinate mitigation and communication, and verify fresh user recovery before learning from the contributing conditions. Explain a concrete example, name an assumption that changes the answer, and sketch the relevant diagram without looking.

How do firing, notified and recovered differ?

A rule condition can fire without delivery; a notification can be delivered without action; user recovery requires separate outcome evidence.

How do grouping and inhibition differ?

Grouping bundles related alerts into notifications; inhibition suppresses selected target notifications while a matched source alert is firing.

Why should the commander avoid doing every diagnosis personally?

Coordination, priority, escalation and handoff are full response duties. Distributed investigations need a clear owner of the shared decision process.

What makes a follow-up actionable?

A concrete system/process change with an owner, priority and verification path, grounded in incident evidence rather than individual blame.

Tricky & Most-Asked Interview Scenarios

One database outage generates five hundred pages. How do you reduce noise safely?

The Scenario & Constraint

Every application instance reports its dependency failure and responders cannot see new unrelated incidents. What routing policy fits?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Group symptoms by meaningful service/environment/incident dimensions and use narrowly matched inhibition for known source/target relationships. Preserve visibility into affected instances and independent incidents while routing to accountable owners.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Grouping by instance defeats consolidation; suppressing an entire organization can hide a second outage. Inhibition controls notifications, not the underlying firing state or service recovery.

An availability alert resolves when the exporter disappears. Is the service healthy?

The Scenario & Constraint

The rule expression returns no series after collection fails. Customer errors may continue. What response fits?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Treat measurement availability as a separate signal, inspect scrape/rule/notification health and use independent user-facing evidence. Define explicit missing/stale-data handling instead of equating absence with success.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Always paging on one missing sample can create noise. A blank chart cannot establish the outcome either way; duration, scope and response policy need to reflect real risk.

A critical page reaches a general inbox instead of the on-call. What do you inspect?

The Scenario & Constraint

A broad matching child route appears before a specialized route and sibling matching stops. What change fits?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Inspect actual labels, matcher order, inherited receiver settings and continue behavior, then test representative alert routes without sending production messages. Ensure a fallback owner and escalation path exist.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Setting continue true everywhere can create duplicates and conflicting ownership. Delivery success to the wrong inbox is not successful incident response, and config validity alone does not test the intended route.

Two teams simultaneously change routing and database settings. Who should coordinate?

The Scenario & Constraint

User impact grows and nobody knows which action caused the latest change. What immediate response fits?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Establish incident command, operations and communication ownership, record current impact/actions, and coordinate bounded changes with evidence and checkpoints. Delegate diagnosis while prioritizing safe harm reduction.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Formal roles should scale to team size rather than add ceremony, but no coordination invites conflicting actions. Waiting for a perfect root cause can prolong avoidable impact.

The quickest rollback may invalidate writes already accepted. What decision fits?

The Scenario & Constraint

A new release is implicated, but its data format differs from the old application. What will the response lead require?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Check compatibility and accepted effects before rollback, select safe containment or a compatible forward/data repair path, and preserve evidence for reconciliation. Separate the need for rapid mitigation from an unsupported claim that all effects are reversible.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Traffic reduction or partial disablement may cost features while avoiding worse data loss. Restoring a backup can discard newer legitimate writes and needs an explicit business recovery decision.

The pager is silent after a broad silence was added. Can the incident close?

The Scenario & Constraint

Backlog remains and telemetry is stale, but no new notification arrives. What closure and learning criteria fit?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Verify fresh user outcomes, backlog/data correctness, delivery health and residual ownership; narrow/remove the silence with an owner/expiry. Close the incident according to impact criteria and create evidence-based follow-up work.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

A quiet notifier is not service recovery. Overlong incident status can distract after mitigation, so hand off explicit residual risks instead of either ignoring them or keeping every task in emergency mode.
