Rolling Deployments

🎯 The Hook & ELI5

A hotel renovates a few rooms at a time while the others stay available. Temporary rooms can keep capacity up, but only if there is space to build them and new rooms are truly ready. Old and new rooms coexist, so the front desk and booking system must understand both.

A rolling deployment incrementally replaces running instances instead of switching a whole environment. It trades temporary spare capacity and replacement speed against availability risk. Kubernetes Deployments provide one concrete implementation for mostly interchangeable Pods through ReplicaSets; stateful workloads have additional identity/storage concerns. Our example replaces ten API replicas while keeping a defined serving budget.

🗺️ High-Level Big Picture

Desired replicas, scheduled Pods and usable serving endpoints are different quantities. A rollout only progresses when its conditions can be satisfied.

🔍 Step-by-Step Mechanics

Follow the worked example one decision at a time. Open each step to connect the everyday model to the implementation; examples state their product/runtime scope explicitly. Trace ownership, state transitions, decision evidence and the failure/recovery boundary.

1. Identify the rollout controller and immutable release

A Kubernetes Deployment owns ReplicaSets that manage Pods matching its template. A changed template, such as a new container image identity, causes replacement; scaling replica count alone is a different action. Keep resource requests, probes, labels and configuration compatible with the intended workload. Editing a running container is not a reproducible release strategy.

2. Translate budgets into concrete counts

For a worked integer-budget example with desired replicas D=10, maxSurge=2 permits up to 12 desired non-terminating replicas during replacement, and maxUnavailable=1 expresses a minimum of 9 available replicas for rollout decisions. These are controller constraints, not a guarantee of 9 successful request streams. Terminating Pods can remain and consume resources during their grace period, so physical usage can temporarily exceed that simple bound. Use documented percentage rounding for your selected Kubernetes version if specifying percentages.

3. Separate scheduling, startup and readiness

The new Pod needs a node that satisfies requests, placement rules, image access and any storage requirements. Startup initialization and readiness take additional time. Readiness gates participation in normal Service traffic; it does not prove all business paths are correct. minReadySeconds can require sustained readiness before a new Pod is considered available for Deployment progress. Healthy HTTP checks must correspond to meaningful safe serving behavior.

4. Handle the mixed-version interval

Old and new replicas may exchange messages and share a database. Preserve API/schema compatibility while they overlap; sequence incompatible cleanup later. Service selection and endpoint propagation have their own timing. For shutdown, budget hooks and in-flight work inside the Pod grace period, stop accepting new work appropriately and bound long connections. Deleting an old Pod is not evidence every client already stopped using its connection.

5. Diagnose a stalled rollout before forcing it

Check Deployment conditions, ReplicaSet counts, Pod events, readiness and node/dependency capacity. ProgressDeadlineExceeded reports a lack of progress; the Deployment does not universally perform automatic rollback merely because the deadline is exceeded. An external deployment tool may implement additional actions. Reverting the Pod template can replace application replicas but cannot undo database changes or previously completed requests. Verify user outcomes after any response.

⚠️ Common Pitfalls, Bugs & What-If Scenarios

Read each failure as a story: diagnose the visible symptom, identify its cause, and change the mechanism that produced it.

No room for the surge

Symptom: Rollout waits with Pending new Pods.

Root Cause: Capacity, quota or affinity cannot fit replacements.

Fix/Prevention: Inspect scheduling evidence and reserve capacity or deliberately revise availability/surge policy.

Ready too early

Symptom: Old replicas disappear before replacements can serve.

Root Cause: Probe checks only process existence or ignores warmup.

Fix/Prevention: Define meaningful readiness/startup behavior and observe real outcomes.

Deadline mistaken for recovery

Symptom: Rollout is marked stalled while bad replicas remain.

Root Cause: An observed progress condition is assumed to trigger automatic application/data reversal.

Fix/Prevention: Implement and test the actual deployment tool’s recovery policy and compatibility boundary.

Explain a concrete example, name an assumption that changes the answer, and sketch the relevant diagram without looking.

How do surge and unavailable budgets differ?

Surge permits temporary replicas above desired count; unavailable permits temporarily fewer available replicas. Scheduling, readiness and termination affect actual serving and resource use.

Does available mean every request succeeds?

No. Controller readiness/availability conditions are limited checks. Real user success and dependency behavior still need observation.

Which compatibility matters during rollout?

Old/new application protocols, database shape, messages and configuration must coexist for the overlap and recovery window.

Does a progress deadline automatically undo a release?

Not by itself in a Kubernetes Deployment. It reports lack of progress; selected higher-level tooling may add a separately configured response.

Tricky & Most-Asked Interview Scenarios

A rollout stalls with healthy old replicas and Pending new Pods. What next?

The Scenario & Constraint

Ten replicas occupy all eligible node capacity, while maxSurge=2 and maxUnavailable=0 leave no replacement room. What evidence and choice fit?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Inspect scheduling events, requests, quota and placement constraints. Add eligible capacity or explicitly accept a controlled availability trade-off before changing the rollout budget; do not delete healthy replicas blindly.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

More nodes cost money and may take time. Allowing unavailable replicas reduces serving headroom. Desired count does not create capacity, and lowering requests without measurement can hide overload.

Rollout is green while customer requests time out. Which check is wrong?

The Scenario & Constraint

A probe returns 200 before a large local index finishes loading. Old replicas are removed as new ones report ready. What change fits?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Make startup/readiness represent safe serving, preserve sufficient capacity during warmup and validate user paths. Use sustained availability criteria where appropriate rather than a process-only probe.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

A slow initialization check must not become an aggressive liveness restart loop. A probe that deeply exercises every dependency can itself overload them; choose meaningful bounded checks.

Only in-flight requests fail as old replicas leave. What will you coordinate?

The Scenario & Constraint

New replicas are healthy, but clients see resets during old Pod termination and some connections are long-lived. What is the response?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Trace endpoint removal propagation, application drain behavior and grace/hook budgets. Stop admitting work, finish or explicitly bound accepted work, and align client retries with idempotency and deadlines.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Increasing grace consumes capacity longer and cannot make unbounded sessions finish. A pre-stop hook uses the same finite shutdown budget; deleting a Pod does not instantly remove every existing connection.

Half the replicas reject records written by the other half. How do you recover?

The Scenario & Constraint

The new release writes an enum value unknown to old code. Rolling replacement intentionally keeps both versions live. What will you change?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Contain incompatible writes, establish a mutually readable representation or compatible release and reconcile affected records. Introduce new formats in staged compatible transitions before later removing old support.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

A full restart may reduce overlap but create downtime and still not repair persisted incompatible data. Reverting an image is not a schema or external-effect rollback.

ProgressDeadlineExceeded appears, yet the bad release remains. Is Kubernetes broken?

The Scenario & Constraint

Operations expects a Deployment to revert itself after the progress deadline. New Pods cannot become ready. What response fits?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Read the actual Deployment/ReplicaSet/Pod conditions, fix the blocking cause or deliberately revert to a compatible template, and verify the selected release automation’s policy. Document that deadline detection and rollback are separate responsibilities.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

A higher-level controller may automate recovery but needs explicit safeguards and data compatibility. Blind recurring rollback can oscillate or reintroduce an obsolete vulnerable version.

Physical Pod usage exceeds desired plus surge during replacement. Why?

The Scenario & Constraint

Long termination grace periods keep old processes alive while new Pods start. Capacity planning assumed only ten plus two processes. What will you inspect?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Distinguish desired, available, running and terminating counts and measure resources retained during drain. Reserve termination overlap capacity and bound shutdown work with the application contract.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Shortening grace saves resources but can drop accepted work. Surge limits do not erase terminating processes immediately, and counting replicas without lifecycle state is an incomplete capacity model.
