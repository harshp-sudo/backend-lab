Fault Tolerance

🎯 The Hook & ELI5

A school keeps a spare bus and practices what to do when the main bus breaks. A spare parked behind the same locked gate may fail to help. The plan must say which failures it covers, how children reach the spare and whether the trip can continue safely while something is missing.

Fault tolerance is the ability to preserve specified useful behavior despite a stated set of faults. It starts with a service promise and failure model, not with adding replicas. Some faults should be masked, some permit degraded behavior, and others require safe refusal and recovery.

Use an order-tracking service spanning two locations. Customers may tolerate an older delivery estimate, but the service must not invent a completed payment. Availability, latency, correctness, durability and recovery time are separate dimensions. A topology that survives one process failure may still fail on shared credentials, a bad deployment, regional loss or corrupted data.

🗺️ High-Level Big Picture

Failover is one step. Recovery also needs correct data, surviving capacity, client routing and verification that normal operation can safely resume.

🔍 Step-by-Step Mechanics

Follow the worked example one decision at a time. Open each step to connect the everyday model to the implementation; examples state their product/runtime scope explicitly. Trace the actual actor, communication, acknowledgment and recovery boundary.

1. Define useful service under named faults

Write the required behavior for a process crash, a lost machine, a partition, a dependency outage and corrupted data. State what is out of scope, such as arbitrary malicious replicas if the design only tolerates crashes. Not every mechanism covers every class of fault.

For order tracking, an explicitly marked older estimate might be acceptable during carrier outage. A definitive payment status may require the payment authority or an honest unknown state. Decide which operations can degrade without misleading users, and make that visible in the interface contract.

2. Separate failure domains and retained state

Replicas provide useful redundancy only if the relevant failure does not remove all of them. Examine host, rack/zone, network, power, credentials, deployments, storage and operational control. Two processes on one machine do not tolerate that machine disappearing.

Replicated state also needs a conflict/authority protocol and retained recovery information. Replication can quickly copy a mistaken deletion or corruption; backups and tested point-in-time recovery address a different problem. Verify the data-loss assumptions under the actual acknowledgment and replication configuration.

3. Budget recovery time and data loss

Recovery time objective describes the targeted restoration time for the specified incident; recovery point objective describes the targeted recoverable data point/acceptable loss window. These are planning objectives to test, not automatic guarantees supplied by a product label.

A restore drill includes obtaining keys/credentials, locating valid backups, restoring state, replaying logs if supported, rebuilding indexes, changing routing and validating business correctness. A five-minute database startup is not proof that the full service meets a five-minute recovery objective.

4. Detect without turning partial failure into total failure

Use bounded health/readiness signals and distinguish a dead process from a slow or isolated dependency. Failover decisions based on imperfect detection can cause competing owners or traffic oscillation. Couple promotion to supported authority/fencing rules where exclusive writes matter.

An optional carrier-estimate dependency should not necessarily make order lookup wholly unavailable. On the other hand, serving a guessed payment result to keep a green dashboard is false availability. Measure failure impact per operation and preserve honest unknown/degraded responses.

5. Provision and admit for the surviving system

If one location fails, surviving capacity must absorb the promised workload or intentionally shed/defer some of it. Calculate downstream connections, cold-cache load, repair traffic and startup delay. A failover that immediately overwhelms the healthy location is a common way redundancy fails in practice.

Use bounded queues/concurrency, priority and graceful feature reduction to protect essential paths. A retry policy can amplify the outage just as failover concentrates traffic. Recovery should ramp traffic deliberately while measuring useful completions and data correctness, not switch everything at once merely because a port responds.

6. Rehearse repair, failback and evidence

Exercise the exact assumed failures in a controlled environment and record expected versus observed outcomes. Include corrupted restore inputs, unavailable secrets, stale clients and a failed region rejoining. Failback needs state reconciliation and authority handoff; it is not just reversing DNS.

Track detection time, safe promotion time, actual restore duration, unrecoverable data, degraded operations, rejected work and repair backlog. Finite tests do not prove all failures harmless, but they reveal missing dependencies and invalidate unsupported recovery claims. Keep runbooks and escalation ownership aligned with current deployments.

⚠️ Common Pitfalls, Bugs & What-If Scenarios

Read each failure as a story: diagnose the visible symptom, identify its cause, and change the mechanism that produced it.

Spare shares the same failure

Symptom: All replicas fail after a credential rotation.

Root Cause: Only hardware redundancy was considered.

Fix/Prevention: Audit shared software, operational and identity dependencies too.

Recovery floods survivors

Symptom: Cold caches and retries overload the remaining region.

Root Cause: Failover capacity ignored transient recovery demand.

Fix/Prevention: Ramp traffic, protect origins and test reduced-capacity operation.

Backups cannot restore

Symptom: Snapshots exist but keys or compatible restore steps are missing.

Root Cause: Backup creation was confused with recovery capability.

Fix/Prevention: Run full restore drills including credentials, integrity and service validation.

🧠 Active Recall & Mastery Check

Summarize this in your own words: Defend a named user promise against named failures, then test independent capacity, authority, data recovery and controlled return to service. Explain a concrete example, name an assumption that changes the answer, and sketch the relevant diagram without looking.

Why state a failure model?

A design can only defend the faults and assumptions it actually covers; replica count alone says little.

How do recovery time and recovery point differ?

One targets restoration duration; the other targets the recoverable data point/loss window for the specified failure.

Why keep backups when data is replicated?

Replication can propagate corruption or deletion, while backups can preserve an independently recoverable earlier state.

What makes degradation acceptable?

The reduced response still meets an explicitly weaker, honest user/business contract.

Tricky & Most-Asked Interview Scenarios

Three replicas disappear together. Was the service fault tolerant?

The Scenario & Constraint

They ran in one failure domain and the requirement covered loss of that domain. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Place enough independent capacity/state across the required domains, inspect shared dependencies and validate the full routing/authority path under a domain outage.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

More domains add cost and coordination latency. Independence must match the assumed failure; separate hosts alone do not address regional or shared-credential failure.

Failover works, then the healthy region collapses. What changes?

The Scenario & Constraint

The surviving region receives normal traffic plus cold-cache loads and retries. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Apply bounded admission/degradation, ramp traffic and test capacity under the full recovery mix. Provision the headroom needed for the promised service level or revise the reduced-service contract.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Reserve capacity costs money and shedding affects users. Adding instances may arrive too late or overload a shared database, so measure downstream and startup constraints.

A bad update reaches every replica. Promote one?

The Scenario & Constraint

All replicas contain the same erroneous deletion and the valid data existed yesterday. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Stop the damaging path, identify a valid recovery point, restore/replay through supported procedures and reconcile later legitimate changes before reopening affected writes.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Historical restoration takes time and may lose/reconcile subsequent work. Another equally corrupted replica cannot restore the missing truth; backup retention and integrity checks are essential.

The payment dependency is down. Show paid to preserve availability?

The Scenario & Constraint

Order tracking can serve some cached information, but payment correctness matters. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Return the permitted order details with an explicit unavailable/unknown payment state and disable actions requiring confirmed payment authority. Use bounded recovery checks.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

A degraded view is less convenient, but inventing success violates the user promise. A signed durable prior payment fact may support a narrower statement if its validity is established.

The backup restores in ten minutes. Is a ten-minute RTO proven?

The Scenario & Constraint

Clients still need keys, DNS/routing changes, log replay and correctness checks. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Measure the end-to-end incident-to-useful-service path with those dependencies and compare the result with the agreed objective. Document where time is spent and close the largest gaps.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Full drills consume resources and coordination. A narrower database-restore metric can still be useful, but must not be advertised as the whole service recovery objective.

The old primary region returns with stale data. Route writes back immediately?

The Scenario & Constraint

The recovery region has accepted authoritative updates during the outage. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Keep one supported authority, reconcile/rebuild the returning state and perform a verified ownership/routing handoff with obsolete writers fenced. Test client caches and delayed packets.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Failback may take longer than connectivity restoration. An active-active merge is an alternative only if its consistency/conflict model fits the data; it cannot be improvised after divergent irreversible actions.
