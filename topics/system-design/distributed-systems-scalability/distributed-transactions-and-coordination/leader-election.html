Leader Election

🎯 The Hook & ELI5

A group of helpers chooses one person to announce the next task. If that helper goes silent, the others need a replacement. But silence may mean a broken radio rather than absence. The old helper can return with outdated instructions, so choosing a new leader and rejecting old instructions are separate responsibilities.

Leader election selects an authority for a defined scope and epoch under explicit assumptions. Safety means forbidden conflicting decisions do not occur; liveness means useful leadership/progress can eventually be established under the required communication/timing conditions. A leader label alone does not replicate data or safely authorize external writes.

Use a shard-placement controller as the running example. One controller decides which worker owns a shard, while several replicas preserve coordination state. Raft is a concrete reference: majority votes, persistent terms/votes and log-safety rules work together. This lesson explains the boundary rather than providing a new consensus implementation.

🗺️ High-Level Big Picture

A new majority does not reach into A and stop its process. Safety at an external worker requires a supported authority/epoch check at that worker’s effect boundary.

🔍 Step-by-Step Mechanics

Follow the worked example one decision at a time. Open each step to connect the everyday model to the implementation; examples state their product/runtime scope explicitly. Trace the actual actor, communication, acknowledgment and recovery boundary.

1. Define the leadership scope and authority

Specify whether the leader owns one replicated log, a scheduler partition, a shard controller or an external task. Narrow independent scopes can reduce bottlenecks, but each scope needs a clear membership and authority contract. Avoid a global leader when independent work has no shared ordering requirement.

For the controller, define a durable ownership assignment and how workers accept it. Election selects who can propose/commit assignments under the protocol; worker-side enforcement determines whether obsolete instructions can still change reality.

2. Understand timeout suspicion

Heartbeats and election timeouts detect an absence of timely communication, not certain process death. An old leader can pause for garbage collection or be isolated while still reaching a storage service. Short timeouts may speed detection but also create avoidable churn under load.

Choose timing from actual network and scheduling behavior, and separate safety from the conditions required for progress. Randomized election timeouts help avoid repeated split votes in Raft; randomness is not a substitute for durable voting or quorum rules.

3. Explain the election protocol as a whole

In Raft, a candidate advances its term, records its vote and asks other members. Each voter persists at most one vote per term and checks candidate log freshness under the protocol. A majority is required. The overlap of majorities supports the argument only together with persistent vote/term and log rules.

Two leaders in different terms can temporarily believe they are leaders; election safety is scoped by term. A node seeing a higher term steps down according to the protocol. It is inaccurate to claim a majority arithmetic slogan physically prevents every old process from acting anywhere.

4. Separate leadership from committed state

Winning an election is not equivalent to every proposed update being committed. Replicated-log commitment and application rules decide when an assignment is durable/visible. A restarted controller must reconstruct authoritative state from the supported store instead of trusting process-local memory.

Likewise, a local watch of coordination state can lag. etcd v3.6 documents separate KV and watch guarantees. If an action needs current authority, use the actual supported transaction/version/lease mechanism and account for an obsolete observer; do not derive an instantaneous global decision from an eventually delivered notification.

5. Fence obsolete external effects

An external resource can require an ownership epoch/token and atomically reject obsolete generations under its defined protocol. A new owner must establish its generation at that boundary before the design relies on excluding older effects. Random owner identity helps identify ownership but is not itself a monotonically ordered generation.

If the protected service cannot enforce authority, election alone may not protect irreversible operations. Consider moving the invariant into a conditional transaction, using provider-supported idempotent intent, or assigning work through a service that can validate generation and operation identity.

6. Manage membership and recovery deliberately

Consensus membership changes require the supported reconfiguration procedure, not editing independent voter lists on several nodes. Adding many unavailable members or letting partitions form incompatible configurations can break assumptions. Backups and disaster recovery must preserve or deliberately reestablish authority with obsolete members fenced.

Observe term changes, leader stability, quorum reachability, commit latency, applied-state lag and worker rejection of old epochs. ⚠️ Common Pitfalls, Bugs & What-If Scenarios

Read each failure as a story: diagnose the visible symptom, identify its cause, and change the mechanism that produced it.

Two active writers after failover

Symptom: An old controller keeps changing external state.

Root Cause: Election was treated as process termination or universal fencing.

Fix/Prevention: Validate generation at the actual effect boundary and test delayed old commands.

Repeated leader churn

Symptom: A loaded cluster elects new leaders continuously.

Root Cause: Timeouts are shorter than realistic scheduling/network delays.

Fix/Prevention: Measure delays and tune the supported protocol with headroom and observability.

Unsafe member replacement

Symptom: Two groups each operate from incompatible voter lists.

Root Cause: Membership was manually redefined without a supported transition.

Fix/Prevention: Follow the coordinator’s reconfiguration/recovery procedure and fence obsolete groups.

🧠 Active Recall & Mastery Check

Summarize this in your own words: Elect authority under a supported protocol, recover committed state, and enforce obsolete-owner rejection where effects actually occur. Explain a concrete example, name an assumption that changes the answer, and sketch the relevant diagram without looking.

What does a missed heartbeat prove?

Only missing timely communication; the old process may still run or access another resource.

Can processes in different terms both believe they lead?

Yes temporarily; protocol and effect-boundary checks must prevent forbidden committed/external decisions.

Why is a majority count alone an incomplete safety argument?

Voting persistence, term rules, candidate/log checks and membership assumptions are also required.

Does winning election automatically fence an external API?

No. The external effect needs its own supported authority/identity mechanism.

Tricky & Most-Asked Interview Scenarios

The old leader resumes after a long pause. What stops its writes?

The Scenario & Constraint

A new leader was elected and workers already accepted a newer ownership epoch. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Require worker/storage mutations to validate the relevant epoch atomically and reject obsolete generations. Make the new owner’s authority installation part of the handoff protocol.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

This requires resource cooperation and durable generation handling. Killing the old process is useful defense but not sufficient for delayed packets; unsupported external effects may need redesign.

A lone replica cannot reach the other two. Should it elect itself?

The Scenario & Constraint

The supported three-member protocol requires a majority and the other pair may still communicate. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Do not invent unilateral authority; preserve the quorum protocol and expose unavailable/pending coordination where needed. Diagnose connectivity and restore supported membership.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

The minority loses coordination availability. An explicitly weaker local display may still work, but it must not mutate authoritative assignments as if it had won an election.

Leaders change every few seconds under load. Shorten the timeout?

The Scenario & Constraint

Process scheduling delays and network tails grow during peak demand. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Correlate election changes with those delays, relieve resource pressure and tune supported timeout ranges against measured behavior rather than shortening them blindly. Test failover and stable peak operation.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Longer timeouts can delay legitimate failover. Separate detection-speed objectives from safety; a timeout does not prove death or fix overloaded coordination storage.

A controller restarts with yesterday’s local shard map. Use it?

The Scenario & Constraint

The authoritative coordination log may contain newer placements. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Recover/read the supported authoritative state and current generation before proposing or acting on assignments; treat local cached state as provisional and reject outdated commands.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Recovery can delay readiness. Using stale state may be acceptable for a read-only historical view, but not for assigning exclusive live ownership under the original promise.

Can both sides replace failed voters independently?

The Scenario & Constraint

A partitioned operations team proposes separate new member lists to restore availability. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Follow one supported membership/recovery procedure with an explicit authority and fencing plan; do not let disconnected groups independently create competing configurations.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Conservative recovery can prolong outage. A deliberate disaster-recovery rebuild may change cluster identity/state, requiring explicit exclusion of obsolete participants and audit of lost updates.

A leader sends a bank transfer and loses leadership before reply. Reissue?

The Scenario & Constraint

The bank cannot validate your election term and the transfer might have completed. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Use the bank’s supported stable intent/idempotency/status mechanism and durable workflow identity, reconciling the original outcome. Do not assume a new leader authorizes a new independent transfer.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Provider cooperation and retained intent state are required. Election orders your control plane, but cannot by itself guarantee exactly-once effects in an unrelated system.
