Distributed Locking

🎯 The Hook & ELI5

A workshop lends one numbered key to the person repairing a machine. The loan expires so an absent person cannot block everyone forever. But the old holder can wake up with the expired key still in their pocket. The machine needs a way to reject an obsolete key, not just a receptionist who knows a new key was lent.

Distributed locking coordinates access across processes/machines under a stated lock-service and failure model. A practical lock often has a lease: ownership expires unless renewed. Expiration improves recovery from abandoned owners, but it does not stop an old process or recall a delayed request.

Separate efficiency locks, where duplicate work is tolerable, from correctness-sensitive exclusion. A cache refresh lease may merely avoid extra computation; an account mutation or overwrite cannot safely depend on the same loose assumption. This lesson focuses on owner identity, lease uncertainty and resource-enforced authority rather than presenting a homemade universal lock algorithm.

🗺️ High-Level Big Picture

The example assumes the resource atomically enforces generations and has observed the new authority. Lease expiration alone cannot revoke an old in-flight request.

🔍 Step-by-Step Mechanics

Follow the worked example one decision at a time. Open each step to connect the everyday model to the implementation; examples state their product/runtime scope explicitly. Trace the actual actor, communication, acknowledgment and recovery boundary.

1. Define the protected invariant and failure model

Ask exactly what duplicates would damage. Two refreshes of a rebuildable public view may waste resources; two conflicting inventory owners can violate a promise. Specify process pauses, partitions, clock/timing assumptions, lock-service crash recovery and whether the protected resource can enforce ownership.

Prefer a supported implementation with documented guarantees. A collection of conditional writes is not automatically a correct distributed lock under every failover model. State the scope of the lock key, including tenant/resource identity, so unrelated objects do not accidentally share or bypass the same boundary.

2. Acquire and release by ownership identity

Acquisition must establish ownership under the actual service semantics, with a unique owner/lease identity. Release must be conditional on still owning that exact lock. A delayed old holder must not delete the new holder’s lock after its own lease expired.

Random owner values help identify “mine” versus “someone else’s” for safe release. They do not establish a total order among ownership generations. Do not confuse a random token, a wall-clock expiry and a monotonic fencing generation; they serve different purposes.

3. Treat renewal as uncertain communication

A lease holder may renew before expiry, but a pause or network failure can prevent timely renewal. A failed renewal response can leave the local process uncertain about the service’s actual state. Stop initiating correctness-sensitive work when authority cannot be established under the supported protocol.

Checking time or ownership immediately before writing still leaves a gap: the process can pause after the check. A longer lease reduces some expirations but increases abandoned-owner recovery time and does not eliminate arbitrarily long pauses or delayed network requests.

4. Enforce authority atomically at the resource

A fencing scheme supplies an increasing ownership generation and makes the resource reject operations from obsolete generations under a defined atomic check. The generation and its comparison must survive the relevant failures. A process-local “latest token” variable is insufficient if another resource replica accepts old writes.

In the diagram B establishes generation 22 before A’s delayed generation 21 arrives. Merely allocating a newer token does not instantly inform every external resource. Define the handoff/validation protocol and whether equal-generation retries require operation IDs or sequencing. Fencing is not a universal duplicate-effect detector.

5. Avoid locks when a smaller atomic primitive fits

If a database owns the protected invariant, a conditional update, uniqueness constraint, row transaction or supported atomic command may combine the decision and mutation more directly. Partition ownership or a durable queue can also reduce shared lock contention where their semantics fit.

A separate lock service adds a network dependency and a second recovery boundary. It can still be useful for coordination, but the protected mutation must remain safe when lock ownership is uncertain. If a provider cannot validate generations or deduplicate intent, the design may need a different workflow rather than a cleverer lease timeout.

6. Operate contention and recovery as normal cases

Bound acquisition waiting and attempts; use deadlines/backoff rather than busy-spinning every contender. Observe acquisition delay, lease loss, renewal uncertainty, hold duration, obsolete-token rejection and protected-operation outcomes. Avoid holding a shared lock across an unpredictable human or third-party delay.

Test pause longer than the lease, lost renewal/release replies, service failover, delayed old writes and a resource restore from backup. Ensure ownership generations do not move backward through restore/reinitialization. The etcd v3.6 lease/API reference and original fencing analysis define concrete boundaries; no live lock cluster is claimed by this illustrative workflow.

⚠️ Common Pitfalls, Bugs & What-If Scenarios

Read each failure as a story: diagnose the visible symptom, identify its cause, and change the mechanism that produced it.

Old holder releases new lock

Symptom: A second worker loses its lock unexpectedly.

Root Cause: Release deleted the key without matching owner identity.

Fix/Prevention: Release atomically only if ownership still matches.

Expired holder overwrites new work

Symptom: A process resumes after another holder completed an update.

Root Cause: Lease expiry was treated as physical cancellation.

Fix/Prevention: Use a supported resource-side authority check or redesign the mutation boundary.

Counter resets after restore

Symptom: An obsolete generation is accepted again.

Root Cause: Fencing state did not survive the documented recovery.

Fix/Prevention: Preserve ordering across recovery or safely reestablish authority while fencing old participants.

🧠 Active Recall & Mastery Check

Summarize this in your own words: A lease coordinates intent; correctness requires owner-safe operations and authority enforced at the protected effect, including pause, failover and restore behavior. Explain a concrete example, name an assumption that changes the answer, and sketch the relevant diagram without looking.

Is a random owner token a fencing generation?

No. It identifies an owner for comparisons such as safe release; it does not necessarily order successive owners.

Why does checking a lease immediately before a write leave a race?

The holder can pause or its request can be delayed after the check and reach the resource after authority changed.

Who enforces fencing?

The actual protected resource must atomically validate the generation/authority under the stated protocol.

Does fencing automatically eliminate every duplicate effect?

No. Same-owner retries and external side effects can still need stable operation identity and deduplication/reconciliation.

Tricky & Most-Asked Interview Scenarios

A worker pauses beyond its lease and overwrites a successor. What changes?

The Scenario & Constraint

Correctness requires obsolete owners not to mutate the shared document. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Use a supported generation/conditional-mutation protocol enforced atomically by the document owner, and establish new authority before relying on obsolete-generation rejection. Test delayed packets as well as process resumes.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

The resource must cooperate and retain authority state. A longer lease reduces frequency but is not a proof against an unbounded pause.

An old unlock deletes a new owner’s lease. How do you fix it?

The Scenario & Constraint

Worker A’s delayed release arrives after worker B acquired the same resource key. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Release only through an atomic owner-identity comparison in the supported lock service, scoped to A’s actual acquisition. Treat an ownership mismatch as no longer owning the lock.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Identity metadata and atomic comparison are required. A separate read then delete can race in the same way; owner-safe release still does not fence A’s old writes.

A renewal request times out. Continue the critical operation?

The Scenario & Constraint

The holder cannot establish whether the lease remains valid and the mutation could corrupt authoritative state. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Stop starting unverified critical work, reconcile through the supported lease protocol, and make the resource enforce authority even for delayed in-flight requests. Preserve unknown outcomes for later reconciliation.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Conservative stopping reduces availability. Expendable duplicate refresh work can choose a weaker efficiency contract, but authoritative effects cannot silently use that assumption.

A third-party API ignores your fencing token. Is the lock enough?

The Scenario & Constraint

A paused old holder can still call the provider after a new holder starts. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Use provider-supported stable intent/idempotency/status semantics or move the invariant to a boundary that can enforce it. If those are unavailable, state the unresolved guarantee and redesign the workflow.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

A separate lock improves coordination but cannot force an unrelated API to reject old authority. Manual reconciliation or a changed business promise may be necessary.

Thousands of callers spin on one lock. What will you change?

The Scenario & Constraint

Acquisition traffic overloads the coordinator and the protected operation takes seconds. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Bound contenders with deadlines/backoff and admission, shorten or remove the critical section where possible, and consider a queue or partitioned ownership suited to the invariant. Measure wait age and useful throughput.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Queueing adds latency and a queue itself needs a capacity/recovery contract. More coordinator replicas do not make one logically exclusive operation parallel.

A restored resource forgets its highest accepted generation. What now?

The Scenario & Constraint

Old delayed operations may still exist and a restored counter is lower than before the incident. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Reestablish a non-regressing authority namespace/generation through the supported recovery procedure and exclude obsolete actors before accepting writes. Include resource and coordinator state in recovery testing.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

A safe reset may require downtime and coordinated fencing. Increasing an in-memory number arbitrarily is not proof that all delayed old generations are excluded.
