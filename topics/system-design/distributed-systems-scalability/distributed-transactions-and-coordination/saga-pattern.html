Saga Pattern

🎯 The Hook & ELI5

Planning a party happens in steps: reserve a room, order food, book a musician. If the musician cancels, you may cancel the room and refund the food order. Those are new actions with their own rules; you cannot pretend nobody saw the original booking or that an already-eaten cake can be uneaten.

A saga organizes a longer business operation into local committed steps and recovery actions. A later failure may trigger compensating steps or forward recovery. It can fit independent service ownership, but intermediate states and compensation semantics are part of the public business contract. It is not an automatic replacement for a required atomic transaction.

Follow an equipment-order workflow: reserve inventory, authorize payment, arrange dispatch. The right order depends on which actions are reversible, which expire and what users may observe. Every command, event and compensation needs a stable workflow/step identity and a durable recovery story.

🗺️ High-Level Big Picture

Recovery actions are separately executed and acknowledged. If voiding or release fails, the saga remains in a recovery state instead of magically rolling back history.

🔍 Step-by-Step Mechanics

Follow the worked example one decision at a time. Open each step to connect the everyday model to the implementation; examples state their product/runtime scope explicitly. Trace the actual actor, communication, acknowledgment and recovery boundary.

1. Define allowed intermediate and final states

Write a business state machine such as pending, inventory-held, payment-authorized, dispatch-requested, completed, compensating and needs-attention. Specify which states a user can see and which actions they may take in each. A pending order is not a final promise of shipment.

Identify semantic invariants at every local owner. For example, inventory reservation is one atomic local decision, while the workflow tracks whether the whole order completed. If the requirement forbids every intermediate cross-service state, a saga may not meet it without changing ownership or the product contract.

2. Make each local step durable and duplicate-safe

Give the workflow and each step a stable identity. The local service should atomically record the step outcome with its business mutation so duplicate delivery can return/reconcile the original result. Do not allocate a fresh payment intent for every transport retry.

A transactional outbox can record local state and the intent to publish the next event together. A relay can redeliver, so the receiver still needs inbox/operation identity and an atomic local effect boundary. Retain these records long enough for the actual retry/replay window; arbitrary deletion can resurrect duplicate effects.

3. Choose orchestration or choreography deliberately

An orchestrator stores progress and issues the next command, centralizing visibility and recovery ownership. It needs durable state, supported restart behavior and a versioning plan. Choreography uses events among services and can reduce direct central control, but the end-to-end dependency and failure chain still exists.

For a small bounded equipment workflow, explicit orchestration can make ownership easier to reason about. Choreography may fit independent reactions with clear event contracts. Compare actual complexity, not a slogan that one has no coupling: schema, ordering and semantic dependencies remain.

4. Design compensation as business behavior

For each completed step, define a compensation only if the action permits it. Voiding an uncaptured authorization differs from refunding a settled charge. Releasing an unused reservation differs from recalling an already dispatched physical item. Choose a point after which forward recovery or manual handling is required.

Compensation must be safe under duplicate delivery and concurrency. It may need an expected state/version so a late cancel cannot undo a newer valid action. Often reverse dependency order is useful, but the domain—not simply reversing a list—determines which corrections preserve meaning.

5. Handle races, timeouts and stuck recovery

A timeout can hide a successful step. Query/retry using its stable identity before deciding whether compensation is required. Persist the transition to compensating and its progress so a restart resumes the same obligation. Use deadlines, retry budgets and an escalation state; a failed refund cannot be erased by logging a warning.

Cancellation can race with a completion event. Resolve the race through supported conditional state transitions and reconcile late events, preserving the actual local outcomes. Ignore, repeat or compensate an event only according to the workflow state and recorded identity, not merely its arrival order.

6. Observe and test the complete lifecycle

Track age in each state, reserved resource expiry, retry counts, duplicate suppression, compensation failures and manual interventions. Test crashes between local commit and publish, duplicate/reordered replies, provider uncertainty and workflow upgrades while old instances remain active.

Use the original Sagas paper for the foundational model and official modern pattern/outbox references for implementation boundaries. The illustrative sequence is not an executed payment-provider integration. Review each external provider’s idempotency, authorization expiry, cancellation and refund rules before making operational claims.

⚠️ Common Pitfalls, Bugs & What-If Scenarios

Read each failure as a story: diagnose the visible symptom, identify its cause, and change the mechanism that produced it.

Compensation called rollback

Symptom: Users saw a charge and its later refund is treated as no charge ever occurred.

Root Cause: Semantic correction was confused with invisible atomic undo.

Fix/Prevention: Document intermediate effects, provider limits and user-visible recovery.

Duplicate charge after retry

Symptom: A timed-out step creates another payment intent.

Root Cause: Step identity changed with each delivery.

Fix/Prevention: Deduplicate/reconcile the same stable intent at the effect boundary.

Cancel undoes a later success

Symptom: An obsolete release event frees inventory for a completed newer order.

Root Cause: The compensation ignored current state/version ownership.

Fix/Prevention: Condition the correction on the exact reservation/step being compensated.

🧠 Active Recall & Mastery Check

Summarize this in your own words: A saga is a durable business workflow with visible intermediate states, duplicate-safe local steps and explicit compensation or forward recovery—not automatic cross-service rollback. Explain a concrete example, name an assumption that changes the answer, and sketch the relevant diagram without looking.

How is compensation different from rollback?

It is a new business action after a local commit, potentially visible, fallible and only semantically corrective.

What identity should survive retries?

The workflow and intended step/effect identity, so the same command is reconciled rather than recreated.

Does an outbox make every saga step exactly once?

No. It protects local publication intent; relay/consumer redelivery still requires duplicate-safe effects.

What does choreography still need?

Explicit event semantics, durable progress/recovery responsibility, observability and resolution of cross-service failures.

Tricky & Most-Asked Interview Scenarios

Payment authorization timed out. Refund immediately?

The Scenario & Constraint

The provider may have accepted the authorization and its reply was lost. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Query or retry the same provider-supported intent identity, establish its actual state and then choose the matching void/refund/recovery path. Keep the order pending until the documented condition is known.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Reconciliation adds latency and provider dependence. Creating a new intent risks duplicate authorization; if status cannot be established, escalate uncertainty rather than inventing a result.

Compensation fails for hours. Is the order canceled?

The Scenario & Constraint

The user was charged, inventory was released and the refund service is unavailable. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Persist a refund/recovery obligation, expose an honest refund-pending status, retry within bounded policy and escalate with an audit trail. Define customer communication and ownership.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Funds remain outstanding and manual operations cost effort. Marking everything canceled may describe order intent but must not falsely claim the financial correction completed.

An inventory-reserved event arrives twice. What changes?

The Scenario & Constraint

The second event would trigger a fresh payment command under the current handler. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Tie event consumption and local step transition to stable workflow/step identity, and use the same payment intent when reconciling duplicate delivery. Store publication intent with local state where required.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Deduplication records require retention and atomic updates. Transport acknowledgments alone cannot prove the external effect happened once; a late replay after retention needs an explicit policy.

A cancel races with dispatch success. Which event wins?

The Scenario & Constraint

Physical dispatch might already be irreversible when a cancel request arrives. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Resolve supported state transitions using recorded workflow/version state, query uncertain dispatch outcomes and choose release, intercept/return or completion according to actual business rules. Preserve late-event reconciliation.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

A simple last-arriving-event rule can misrepresent physical reality. Stronger serialization at the dispatch boundary may reduce ambiguity but cannot retract an already completed external action.

Event reactions form a cycle and nobody owns the stuck order. What now?

The Scenario & Constraint

Several services emit compensating events and progress is hard to reconstruct. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Define the end-to-end state/event graph and a recovery owner, add correlation/step identities and transition guards, and consider explicit orchestration if it makes this workflow easier to operate.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

An orchestrator adds a component and does not remove event delivery problems. Choreography remains viable if its contracts, cycle prevention and recovery responsibilities are explicit.

Can an order-confirmed email be compensated invisibly?

The Scenario & Constraint

The email is sent before the last required business step and later the saga fails. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Send final confirmation only after the required completion state, or label earlier messages as provisional. If already sent, use a clear correction/cancellation message and record the communication outcome.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Waiting delays communication, while provisional messages complicate UX. No database compensation can unsend a delivered email; changing event order must also preserve durable publication.
