Distributed Transactions

🎯 The Hook & ELI5

Booking a school trip needs both a bus and museum tickets. Two clerks have separate books. “I booked mine” from one clerk does not mean the trip is ready. You can arrange a protocol that decides together, or allow individual bookings and explicitly cancel/recover when the rest fails.

A distributed transaction coordinates a unit of work involving more than one participant or transactional resource. The central question is which invariant must hold, when success can be acknowledged, and how participants recover after messages or processes fail. Multiple database connections alone do not define a safe atomic boundary.

Use an internal credit transfer: debit one account owner and credit another. If the product requires both changes atomically visible under a specified isolation guarantee, that is a stronger contract than a transfer workflow allowed to remain pending while reconciliation completes. Atomic commit, isolation, durability and external side effects must each be stated.

🗺️ High-Level Big Picture

The first decision is the required business promise. A protocol name cannot decide whether a pending transfer or compensation is acceptable.

🔍 Step-by-Step Mechanics

Follow the worked example one decision at a time. Open each step to connect the everyday model to the implementation; examples state their product/runtime scope explicitly. Trace the actual actor, communication, acknowledgment and recovery boundary.

1. Write the invariant and visibility requirement

For a hypothetical internal transfer of ten credits, define whether total balance must remain constant in every externally visible state, whether temporary held funds are allowed, and which reads must observe the transfer atomically. Identify each state owner and every external effect.

An API may return accepted with a durable transfer ID before completion if that is its documented contract. It must not return final success while one side is only an in-memory intention. Distinguish command acceptance, commit decision, participant application and user-visible completion.

2. Try a simpler ownership boundary first

If both balances can safely share one transactional database/partition, a local transaction often makes invariant enforcement and recovery simpler. This does not mean one database is always possible: ownership, capacity, regulatory placement and organizational boundaries may matter.

Alternatively model a transfer as movement into an explicit pending/escrow state with defined ownership. That changes the workflow contract and may reduce the coordination needed at one instant. Do not secretly weaken “both immediately visible” into “we will repair it later” while keeping the same success wording.

3. Understand what atomic commit actually answers

An atomic commit protocol decides whether all required participants commit or abort under its assumptions. Classic two-phase commit asks participants to prepare and then disseminates a durable decision. Prepared participants may retain resources and wait while the decision is unavailable. The detailed 2PC lesson develops that failure state.

Atomic commit alone is not the whole isolation story. A complete distributed transaction implementation also needs a supported concurrency-control/snapshot model, durable logs and recovery rules. Nor does agreement on a database outcome automatically include a third-party payment or email API that cannot participate.

4. Make local state and publication consistent

Suppose a transfer workflow commits a pending record and then publishes an event. A crash between those operations can leave accepted work undispatched. A transactional outbox stores local state and publication intent in one supported local transaction; a relay later publishes it.

The relay can deliver duplicates if it crashes after sending but before recording progress. Consumers still need stable event/operation identity and duplicate-safe state changes within their own atomic boundary. The outbox closes a particular local dual-write gap, not every cross-service atomicity or ordering problem.

5. Recover unknown and partial outcomes

A coordinator can decide commit and lose its reply; a participant can apply a decision and lose its acknowledgment. Treat timeouts as uncertainty. Use durable operation IDs, decision/status lookup and idempotent completion handling so retrying the same intent does not create a second transfer.

For workflow-based transfers, persist every state transition and compensation/retry obligation. Assign a recovery owner, deadlines, reconciliation and manual escalation for cases that cannot automatically finish. Do not let a repair process issue a contradictory decision to a participant already committed.

6. Operate and test the chosen promise

Measure prepared transaction age, held resources, workflow backlog, retries, unresolved outcomes and invariant violations. Test crashes before and after each durable boundary, not only before and after the entire happy path. Include lost and duplicate messages and long coordinator unavailability.

Document the exact product/version/protocol if making executable claims. PostgreSQL 18 prepared transactions are participant facilities intended for an external transaction manager; a hand-written pair of prepare commands is not a production distributed transaction system. Prefer supported implementations over an improvised coordinator.

⚠️ Common Pitfalls, Bugs & What-If Scenarios

Read each failure as a story: diagnose the visible symptom, identify its cause, and change the mechanism that produced it.

One connection commits first

Symptom: A debit remains after credit failure.

Root Cause: Two independent local commits were called one transaction.

Fix/Prevention: Use the actual required atomic protocol or an explicit pending/recovery model.

Published event disappears

Symptom: A committed transfer never reaches the worker.

Root Cause: Database commit and message publication had a crash gap.

Fix/Prevention: Store publication intent with local state and handle relay redelivery.

Recovery creates a second transfer

Symptom: The client retries a timed-out command with a new identity.

Root Cause: Outcome uncertainty was mistaken for proof of failure.

Fix/Prevention: Retain stable intent identity and reconcile its existing status.

What determines whether a saga is a valid substitute?

Whether its intermediate states, compensation and completion timing meet the actual business contract.

Does atomic commit define all transaction isolation?

No. Coordinated outcome and permitted concurrent visibility are distinct parts of a complete implementation.

What narrow problem does an outbox address?

The crash gap between a local transactional state change and recording the intent to publish its corresponding event.

What should a timeout mean to recovery logic?

The outcome may be unknown; inspect durable identity/status rather than assume nothing happened.

Tricky & Most-Asked Interview Scenarios

Debit commits but credit fails. What do you change?

The Scenario & Constraint

The API promised a final atomic transfer, but services independently commit their own databases. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Revisit ownership and use one supported atomic boundary or distributed transaction meeting the required visibility/failure contract. If the business instead approves a pending-transfer workflow, change its state and acknowledgment semantics explicitly.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Distributed commit adds coupling and can retain resources during failure. Compensation is an alternative only if the business accepts the intermediate and recovered outcomes.

A committed order has no event. How do you prevent recurrence?

The Scenario & Constraint

The process crashes after local commit and before broker publication. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Atomically store the state change and outbox entry locally, relay it durably, and make consumer effects duplicate-safe using stable identity. Reconcile already stranded records.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

The relay adds lag and operational backlog. A broker transaction does not automatically include an unrelated database; the outbox also does not make all downstream effects atomic.

Can your transaction include a provider that only offers HTTP charge?

The Scenario & Constraint

The provider cannot prepare or join your commit protocol. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Model the external action as a recoverable workflow with the provider’s documented idempotency/status/refund semantics, or change the product promise. Keep local acceptance and final outcome distinct.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Refunding is not erasing a charge, and some effects are irreversible. If no safe reconciliation exists, escalation or a different provider/operation order may be required.

The coordinator times out after deciding commit. Should clients start over?

The Scenario & Constraint

A durable transfer ID exists, but its final response was lost. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Look up or retry the same operation through supported status/identity semantics, allowing recovery to disseminate the existing decision. Do not create a fresh transfer merely because the response disappeared.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Status retention and reconciliation add storage/latency. A definite pre-acceptance rejection permits a new attempt, but a communication timeout alone does not establish that condition.

All participants commit, yet readers see an invalid combination. Why?

The Scenario & Constraint

Atomic outcome was implemented, but readers use independent snapshots and no joint isolation contract. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Specify and implement the required cross-object visibility/concurrency-control guarantee, or expose a versioned/pending view that honestly reflects the workflow. Test concurrent readers as well as commit outcomes.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Stronger isolation can add waiting and distributed coordination. If the application only needs eventual reporting, a weaker explicitly stated view may suffice; atomic commit alone cannot answer the read requirement.

A pending transfer is stuck for hours. Who fixes it?

The Scenario & Constraint

Every service retries locally, but none owns reconciliation across the workflow. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Assign a durable workflow/recovery owner with an explicit state machine, deadlines, idempotent steps, reconciliation checks and manual escalation. Inspect the actual durable outcomes before acting.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Central recovery ownership adds an operational component; choreography still needs equivalent responsibility and observability. Blindly replaying all actions can duplicate external effects or contradict completed steps.
