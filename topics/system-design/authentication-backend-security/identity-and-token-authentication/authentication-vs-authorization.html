Authentication vs Authorization

🎯 The Hook & ELI5

At a swimming club, showing your membership card answers “Who are you?” It does not answer “May you enter the staff room?” The door policy must check that particular room and action. A visitor cannot become a lifeguard by writing “lifeguard” on their own request slip.

Authentication establishes an identity or authenticated context under an explicit assurance model. Authorization decides whether that principal may perform an action on a resource under current policy. They solve connected but separate problems: a correctly authenticated customer can still be forbidden from another customer’s invoice.

Follow Alice requesting an invoice in a multi-tenant billing service. The service needs a trusted principal, the invoice’s authoritative tenant/owner and a policy for the requested action. Shape validation, a successful login, an obscure invoice ID and a hidden interface button cannot substitute for that decision. The running example concerns a protected resource; genuinely public resources can have a deliberate anonymous-access policy.

🗺️ High-Level Big Picture

Identity is an input to the permission decision. The resource owner must enforce the actual action and scope, including calls that did not traverse the expected user interface.

🔍 Step-by-Step Mechanics

Follow the worked example one decision at a time. Open each step to connect the everyday model to the implementation; examples state their product/runtime scope explicitly. Trace the actor, trusted input, enforcement point and recovery boundary.

1. Define the principal and its assurance

A principal can be a person, workload or delegated client. Establish it through a supported authentication mechanism and retain enough provenance to interpret its claims. A browser-supplied userId or tenant header is a request parameter, not proof of identity.

Successful authentication is also not always sufficient assurance for every action. A sensitive credential change may require recent reauthentication or stronger factors under the product’s policy. Keep identity verification, session continuity and action-specific assurance explicit rather than treating “logged in” as one permanent boolean.

2. Name the action and authoritative resource

ReadInvoice and RefundInvoice can have different policies even for the same object. Resolve the invoice’s tenant and ownership from trusted application data, not from the caller’s assertion that invoice 123 belongs to their tenant. Constrain lookups to an authorized scope where practical.

Guess-resistant identifiers can reduce accidental disclosure but do not authorize access. Also protect alternate representations, batch exports, downloads and administrative paths. A list endpoint and an object endpoint must implement compatible scoping so a secure detail view is not undermined by an unfiltered export.

3. Evaluate policy with trustworthy current inputs

Map trusted roles, attributes or relationships to the intended action/resource decision. Deny unless an explicit policy permits the operation, and handle missing or invalid required facts safely. A policy-engine outage must not silently turn into universal access.

Define the allowed staleness of identity memberships and permission caches. Different actions may justify different freshness/availability trade-offs, but the fallback must be part of the policy. A cached allowance is still an authorization decision with a version/lifetime, not an escape from enforcing authorization.

4. Put enforcement at the real owner

A gateway can verify credentials and apply broad admission controls. The domain service still owns invoice-specific checks and must not be bypassable by direct internal calls. Workload authentication may identify the calling service while delegated user authorization identifies the person/action it may represent.

Do not trust arbitrary identity headers arriving through another route. Establish protected propagation between trusted components, strip untrusted impersonation fields and preserve the intended user/service distinction. Internal network placement alone does not establish permission to access every tenant’s data.

5. Coordinate policy changes and effects honestly

A permission check can become stale before a delayed or concurrent effect commits. State whether the product accepts bounded staleness or needs an authoritative permission/version check coordinated with the sensitive mutation. Use the chosen store/workflow’s real concurrency boundary rather than claiming a separate preflight makes time stop.

For queued work, decide whether authorization is checked at submission, execution or both and what happens if access is revoked between them. The original actor and delegated scope must survive the handoff without granting a worker unrestricted authority merely because it is trusted infrastructure.

6. Test denial and retain safe diagnostic context

Test another tenant’s object, a valid principal with the wrong action, missing claims, direct backend access, privilege removal and dependency failure. A positive test proving Alice can read her own invoice does not establish that she cannot read Bob’s.

Record safe principal/resource/action/policy-version decision context with controlled access and redacted credentials. Public error detail must balance correction and object-existence disclosure. No identity service or live access-control engine is executed here; the traces define the checks a real implementation must prove.

⚠️ Common Pitfalls, Bugs & What-If Scenarios

Read each failure as a story: diagnose the visible symptom, identify its cause, and change the mechanism that produced it.

A member reads another tenant’s invoice

Symptom: A valid session reaches an arbitrary invoice ID.

Root Cause: Authentication was treated as authorization.

Fix/Prevention: Enforce trusted tenant/resource/action policy at the owning operation.

The internal endpoint bypasses policy

Symptom: Requests denied at the gateway succeed directly.

Root Cause: The backend assumed every caller had passed identical edge checks.

Fix/Prevention: Protect identity propagation and enforce resource policy on every path.

Revoked access persists unexpectedly

Symptom: A removed user still performs sensitive actions.

Root Cause: Token/cache lifetime exceeded the promised revocation freshness.

Fix/Prevention: Define and enforce freshness/version invalidation at the actual effect boundary.

🧠 Active Recall & Mastery Check

Summarize this in your own words: Carry trusted identity to the owner of the real resource/action, enforce permission under an explicit freshness and failure policy, and test denied paths as carefully as successful ones. Explain a concrete example, name an assumption that changes the answer, and sketch the relevant diagram without looking.

What different questions do authentication and authorization answer?

Who or what the caller is under the authentication contract, and whether that principal may perform this action on this resource.

Does an unguessable object ID establish permission?

No. Permission still depends on trusted resource scope and action policy.

Why is a gateway check insufficient for every domain action?

The service owns resource-specific facts and alternate call paths; edge credential acceptance is only part of the trust chain.

What happens to cached permissions after removal?

They remain stale unless the system’s explicit lifetime/invalidation/version policy makes the promised revocation effective.

Tricky & Most-Asked Interview Scenarios

A valid customer fetches another tenant’s invoice. Where is the fix?

The Scenario & Constraint

The token is genuine but the endpoint accepts any invoice ID. Tenant separation is required. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Resolve and authorize the object/action within trusted tenant scope at the billing owner, test list/download/batch paths and reject untrusted tenant assertions.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Scoped queries and policy checks add work. Random IDs reduce guessing but cannot replace authorization; a legitimate cross-tenant administrator needs an explicit scoped policy.

The gateway denies a request but the internal service accepts it. What changes?

The Scenario & Constraint

A direct backend route trusts caller-supplied identity headers. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Establish authenticated/protected propagation, discard spoofable headers and enforce the domain action/resource policy on all backend paths.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Repeated checks and context propagation cost effort. A network boundary can reduce exposure but cannot prove every internal caller has the requested tenant permission.

A removed role still permits refunds. What is the revocation contract?

The Scenario & Constraint

Cached claims outlive the promised immediate permission change. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

For the sensitive refund, consult authoritative current policy or enforce a supported policy/version invalidation boundary, then align token/cache lifetimes with that promise.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Fresh checks add latency and outage coupling. Bounded stale access may fit low-risk views, but must not be advertised as immediate revocation.

The policy service times out. Should every authenticated request pass?

The Scenario & Constraint

This action accesses private invoices and requires a current permission decision. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Deny or return a controlled unavailable outcome without performing the protected action; recover the dependency and expose safe diagnostics.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Availability is reduced. A deliberately bounded cached-policy fallback can suit another documented risk model, but a timeout cannot manufacture permission.

A user loses access while their export waits in a queue. Can it run?

The Scenario & Constraint

The export contains private data and execution occurs hours after submission. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Define execution-time authorization and delegated scope, recheck required current access before producing/releasing private output and give revoked work a discoverable terminal outcome.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Rechecks add policy dependencies and can cancel previously accepted work. Submission-only authorization is a different explicit contract with a longer exposure window.

A stolen active session changes recovery credentials. Was login enough?

The Scenario & Constraint

The operation is high-impact and the product supports recent stronger authentication. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Require the action’s configured recent reauthentication/stronger assurance, authorize the account change and rotate/revoke affected session state under a recoverable policy.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Additional friction can interrupt users. Stronger authentication does not replace object/action authorization or make an already compromised browser harmless.
