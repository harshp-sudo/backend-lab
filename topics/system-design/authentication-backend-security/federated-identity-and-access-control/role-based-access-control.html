Role-Based Access Control (RBAC)

🎯 The Hook & ELI5

At a theatre, an usher, cashier and stage manager receive different bundles of duties. Assigning Maya the cashier role is easier than listing every cash-desk action again. But being a cashier at one theatre does not make Maya a cashier at every theatre, and selling a ticket does not automatically include approving her own refund.

Role-Based Access Control groups permissions into roles and assigns users or principals to those roles. The role is an administrative abstraction over allowed operations, not a magic string that bypasses resource scope or current policy. NIST’s historical RBAC model provides the role/permission, assignment, hierarchy and separation-of-duties foundation used here.

Consider a multi-tenant invoicing application with viewer, editor and refund-approver responsibilities. Define whether each permission covers a tenant, project or individual object. Simple roles can be useful, while contextual ownership or transaction constraints may require additional attributes/relationships. This lesson does not claim every permission problem should be encoded as another role.

🗺️ High-Level Big Picture

A role assignment has scope and lifecycle. The same role name in another tenant is a different assignment; additional business constraints remain at the action’s enforcement boundary.

🔍 Step-by-Step Mechanics

Follow the worked example one decision at a time. Open each step to connect the everyday model to the implementation; examples state their product/runtime scope explicitly. Trace the actor, trusted input, enforcement point and recovery boundary.

1. Model actions before naming roles

Inventory meaningful operations such as invoice.read, invoice.edit and refund.approve. Decide which resources and tenant boundaries they apply to, then group permissions into job-oriented roles. Avoid starting with a global isAdmin flag that silently means every privileged operation forever.

Roles should make administration understandable. Keep sensitive permissions separate when their risk and approval duties differ. A read-only support role need not include exporting every customer’s personal data merely because both operations display information.

2. Scope membership and assignment explicitly

This dependency-free Python 3 model demonstrates only tenant-scoped role-to-permission lookup. Inputs represent an already authenticated principal and server-resolved resource; it does not authenticate requests, implement a policy engine or make concurrent revocation atomic. The viewer permission intentionally covers invoices within its assigned tenant.

PERMISSIONS = {
    "viewer": {"invoice.read"},
    "editor": {"invoice.read", "invoice.edit"},
}
MEMBERSHIPS = {("alice", "tenant-a"): {"viewer"}}

def can(user, action, resource):
    tenant = resource.get("tenant")
    if tenant is None:
        return False
    roles = MEMBERSHIPS.get((user, tenant), set())
    return any(action in PERMISSIONS.get(role, set()) for role in roles)

assert can("alice", "invoice.read", {"tenant": "tenant-a"})
assert not can("alice", "invoice.edit", {"tenant": "tenant-a"})
assert not can("alice", "invoice.read", {"tenant": "tenant-b"})
assert not can("alice", "invoice.read", {})
assert not can("unknown", "invoice.read", {"tenant": "tenant-a"})
print("scoped role model: passed")

The important lookup key is principal plus scope, not a client-supplied role string. A production service resolves the resource and policy from trusted data, protects assignment changes and handles persistence, cache/version and denial behavior under its actual implementation contract.

3. Treat hierarchy and separation of duties as policy

A hierarchy can let a senior role inherit explicitly defined permissions. Verify the direction of inheritance and prevent cycles/accidental broadening in the chosen model. A name such as manager does not prove a safe hierarchy; inspect the actual granted operations.

Separation of duties can prohibit incompatible assignments or require distinct actors for an operation. A refund approver may not approve the refund they initiated even though they possess approval permission. Define whether that is an assignment constraint, activation constraint or resource/workflow check and enforce it where it can actually hold.

4. Enforce policy on every action path

Use trusted membership and permission mapping at the owning service for detail/list/batch/export and background paths. UI visibility can guide users but cannot prevent a direct API request. Gateway authentication and a signed role claim do not automatically resolve current tenant-specific permissions.

Protect administrative role-assignment APIs especially carefully; otherwise a perfectly enforced role table can still be rewritten by an unauthorized user. Audit who changed which assignment and why, without exposing credentials. Break-glass/support access needs explicit scope, expiration and review rather than an undocumented global bypass.

5. Plan role change and cache freshness

Removing a role must become effective within the promised policy window. A long-lived token, local permission cache or queued task can retain old authority. Use suitable version/expiry/invalidation/current checks, with stricter freshness for consequential actions where required.

Do not claim that deleting one role row immediately cancels every in-flight effect. Coordinate current permission with the mutation under a supported authoritative boundary when necessary, or state the accepted consistency window. A policy-store outage should not turn unknown role membership into an allowance.

6. Control role growth and verify denials

If every customer/document/time condition creates a new role, management can become harder than the original permission list. Keep stable job permissions in roles and use explicit attributes/relationships for contextual rules when justified. Hybrid designs need one understandable policy, not contradictory enforcement layers.

Test cross-tenant access, missing/unknown roles, assignment escalation, inheritance, self-approval, role removal and stale caches. The local model tests a small permission mapping only; no live role administration, concurrent database or token stack is executed. Review effective permissions, not just the existence of a role name.

⚠️ Common Pitfalls, Bugs & What-If Scenarios

Read each failure as a story: diagnose the visible symptom, identify its cause, and change the mechanism that produced it.

Tenant A administrator controls tenant B

Symptom: The role name is accepted without assignment scope.

Root Cause: Global role text replaced a trusted tenant membership lookup.

Fix/Prevention: Bind assignments and resource resolution to the intended tenant/project.

An approver signs their own refund

Symptom: Approval permission is checked but distinct-actor policy is not.

Root Cause: Role possession was treated as the entire workflow invariant.

Fix/Prevention: Enforce separation-of-duties at the supported assignment/action boundary.

Hundreds of near-identical roles appear

Symptom: Every document condition creates another role.

Root Cause: Contextual policy was forced into static permission bundles.

Fix/Prevention: Keep job roles coherent and introduce explicit attributes/relationships where justified.

🧠 Active Recall & Mastery Check

Summarize this in your own words: Use roles to organize explicit scoped permissions, protect assignment and enforcement, and keep freshness and workflow constraints visible rather than hiding them inside a role name. Explain a concrete example, name an assumption that changes the answer, and sketch the relevant diagram without looking.

What two mappings define the basic role abstraction?

Principals are assigned roles, and roles are assigned permissions, within an explicitly defined scope.

Why is role name equality insufficient across tenants?

The assignment and resource scope define authority; identical names can belong to unrelated tenant memberships.

Does approval permission allow approving your own request?

Only if the workflow permits it; separation-of-duties can impose a distinct-actor condition beyond role possession.

Why does role removal need a freshness design?

Tokens, caches and queued/in-flight work may retain earlier permission until the supported invalidation/current-check contract takes effect.

Tricky & Most-Asked Interview Scenarios

An admin in tenant A edits tenant B. What must change?

The Scenario & Constraint

The API trusts a role claim but does not resolve its tenant assignment. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Bind trusted role assignment to the requested resource’s authoritative tenant and enforce action permission at the owner, including batch/admin paths.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Scoped administration adds data checks. A legitimate cross-tenant support capability needs a separate explicit limited policy rather than accidental global admin text.

A refund approver approves their own refund. Which check is missing?

The Scenario & Constraint

The business requires two distinct people for this workflow. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Enforce the relevant separation-of-duties constraint against the request’s trusted initiator and approving actor at the supported action boundary.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Additional workflow state and coordination cost time. Splitting role names helps administration but cannot alone prove two different actors performed the steps.

Every project/status combination needs a new role. Should you keep adding them?

The Scenario & Constraint

Administrators can no longer explain effective permissions reliably. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Retain stable job permission bundles and model project/status constraints through an explicit contextual or relationship policy with tested combining behavior.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

A hybrid adds evaluation inputs and tests. Pure RBAC remains simpler where duties are stable and context truly does not change permission.

Role removal takes hours to affect exports. What is the policy?

The Scenario & Constraint

The promised revocation window is shorter than cached token/role data lifetime. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Align invalidation/version/current checks with the required export authorization boundary and test stale/queued requests after removal.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Fresh checks add latency and dependency coupling. Short cache TTL reduces but does not equal immediate revocation; document the actual window.

A user grants themselves editor through a hidden endpoint. Where is the defect?

The Scenario & Constraint

Read/edit enforcement is correct but the assignment API lacks protected administration policy. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Authorize role-management actions separately, validate permitted assignment scope and audit changes, then reconcile unauthorized grants and downstream effects.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Administration policy adds controls and support work. Hiding the button or endpoint from documentation does not protect a callable privileged transition.

The role service returns an unfamiliar role after deployment. Should the API allow access?

The Scenario & Constraint

A required private action has no recognized permission mapping. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Deny that action under the default-deny policy, expose safe diagnostics and fix the versioned mapping/rollout before granting it deliberately.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Denying can disrupt legitimate new roles. A coordinated staged rollout is an alternative; a wildcard allow fallback silently grants undefined authority.
