Encryption at Rest & In Transit

🎯 The Hook & ELI5

A locked delivery van protects papers while they travel, and a locked filing cabinet protects them while stored. The clerk who opens both can still read them. A cabinet key left inside the same stolen box may add little protection, and a lost only key can make legitimate records unreadable forever.

Encryption protects confidentiality within a defined data/key and attacker boundary. Encryption in transit protects a network hop between authenticated endpoints; encryption at rest protects stored representations under a storage/key model. Neither automatically authorizes an application user or prevents an already authorized compromised application from reading plaintext.

Follow a customer document from browser to edge proxy, application, database and backup. We use current OWASP TLS/cryptographic-storage guidance conceptually, with no deployed certificate authority, cipher implementation or cloud KMS. Product-specific algorithms, key APIs, nonce constraints and compliance claims must be checked for the real platform instead of inferred from an “encrypted” checkbox.

🔍 Step-by-Step Mechanics

Follow the worked example one decision at a time. Open each step to connect the everyday model to the implementation; examples state their product/runtime scope explicitly. Trace the actor, trusted input, enforcement point and recovery boundary.

1. Name data, adversary and required recovery

Classify what must be protected and from whom: lost disks, stolen backups, network interception, an overprivileged database reader or a compromised application are different threats. Minimize retained sensitive data when it is unnecessary; encryption still leaves metadata and access-pattern questions.

Decide who must recover plaintext, for how long and under what availability policy. Password verification usually needs one-way password hashing, not recoverable password encryption. A design that requires data recovery must include key retention/backup and authorized restore tests, not just successful initial encryption.

2. Protect each network hop and authenticate its endpoint

Use a supported TLS stack/configuration and validate the expected peer certificate/name/trust chain according to the client/platform. Encryption to the wrong endpoint does not meet the intended identity boundary. Avoid disabling verification to silence certificate rotation or hostname errors.

If TLS terminates at an edge, the edge sees plaintext. Choose and verify protection/authentication for subsequent hops; a private subnet is not the same statement as an authenticated protected connection. Client certificates can add a supported workload identity boundary where appropriate, but do not by themselves grant arbitrary application permissions.

3. Choose storage-layer versus field/application protection

Disk/database-managed encryption can protect storage media and snapshots under the provider’s key model while transparently serving plaintext to authorized queries. It does not prevent an application/SQL account with read permission from retrieving those rows. Field/application encryption can establish a different key-access boundary when justified.

Field encryption changes search, indexing, joins and operational support. Identify where decryption occurs and who can invoke it. Keep logs, caches, exports, temporary files and backups within the data lifecycle; protecting only the main database file leaves other plaintext copies exposed.

4. Use authenticated primitives under a supported key/nonce contract

Use maintained high-level cryptographic libraries or managed encryption services. Authenticated encryption protects confidentiality and detects unsupported modification under its actual key/nonce/tag rules. Encryption without integrity can leave important manipulation attacks outside its protection.

Nonce uniqueness/usage requirements are algorithm-specific; for modes requiring non-reuse under one key, concurrency, restart and restore must not accidentally repeat that combination. Bind relevant nonsecret context with supported authenticated-data features where appropriate, and reject failed authentication rather than returning partially trusted plaintext. Do not invent a cipher or copy a nonce constant into production.

5. Separate data keys from key-encryption authority

An envelope design can encrypt data with data keys and protect those keys under a separately controlled key-encryption key/service. This separates bulk data processing from key-control policy, but the application’s ability to unwrap/decrypt remains powerful authority. Apply least privilege and audit its use.

Key rotation can mean changing a wrapping key, rewrapping data keys or reencrypting data; these are not automatically identical operations. Verify what the provider’s rotation actually changes, which old keys remain required and how restored backups find their key versions. No vendor-specific automatic re-encryption guarantee is assumed here.

6. Recover safely through key and certificate changes

Test expired/wrong certificates, key-service denial/outage, missing historical keys, modified ciphertext, failed authentication and backup restore. Define how long usable keys may be cached and who can access plaintext while a key service is unavailable. Bound any cache and preserve the access/expiry threat model.

If a required key cannot be obtained, a controlled unavailable outcome is safer than bypassing verification or storing plaintext as an emergency fallback. Irrecoverable key loss can mean irrecoverable data loss; a compromised decryption key may require data/credential incident response beyond merely issuing a new key. ⚠️ Common Pitfalls, Bugs & What-If Scenarios

Read each failure as a story: diagnose the visible symptom, identify its cause, and change the mechanism that produced it.

HTTPS is advertised as end-to-end

Symptom: The edge forwards private data over an unprotected backend link.

Root Cause: Only the browser-facing hop was assessed.

Fix/Prevention: Map termination/plaintext points and verify each required subsequent connection.

Encrypted database still leaks through the app

Symptom: A stolen application identity reads every sensitive row.

Root Cause: At-rest protection was mistaken for application authorization.

Fix/Prevention: Restrict access and consider a justified separate field/key boundary.

Old backups cannot be restored

Symptom: Current data works but the only old decryption key was retired.

Root Cause: Data retention and key-version lifetime were planned separately.

Fix/Prevention: Retain/protect required key material and test historical recovery before retirement.

🧠 Active Recall & Mastery Check

Summarize this in your own words: Trace plaintext, ciphertext and decryption authority across every hop and copy. Use supported authenticated protection, and make key availability, rotation and historical recovery part of the data contract. Explain a concrete example, name an assumption that changes the answer, and sketch the relevant diagram without looking.

Why name the attacker before choosing encryption?

Different layers protect different exposure paths; stolen media, network interception and compromised authorized software are not the same boundary.

What does TLS termination reveal?

The terminating endpoint can see plaintext; later hops need their own required protection and authentication.

Why use a supported authenticated-encryption interface?

Confidentiality alone does not establish ciphertext integrity; the actual key/nonce/tag contract must be enforced.

Does rotating a wrapping key necessarily reencrypt all data?

No. Wrapping-key rotation, data-key rewrapping and bulk data re-encryption are different operations under the provider’s contract.

Tricky & Most-Asked Interview Scenarios

The site uses HTTPS but backend traffic is readable. What do you change?

The Scenario & Constraint

Sensitive data crosses an edge termination and another network hop. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Map plaintext/termination points and protect/authenticate each required hop with supported TLS configuration and trust validation.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Extra TLS/certificate operations add cost. A private subnet can reduce exposure but does not itself authenticate or encrypt that hop.

The database is encrypted, yet a stolen service account reads private records. Why?

The Scenario & Constraint

That account is authorized to query plaintext through the database. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Restrict application/data/key authority, investigate the compromise and consider a justified field/key-access boundary if the threat model requires separation from that reader.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Field encryption complicates queries and operations. Disk encryption still protects the media threat; it never promised denial to every authorized application identity.

A restarted encryptor reuses nonce values under the same key. Is a random-looking output enough?

The Scenario & Constraint

The selected authenticated mode requires the documented key/nonce usage invariant. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Stop unsafe issuance, assess affected data and use the supported library/service nonce/key lifecycle that remains correct across concurrency/restarts/restores.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Safer allocation or managed encryption adds state/operations. A visually different ciphertext or successful decryption does not prove the nonce invariant held.

The key service is down. Save plaintext temporarily to keep writes running?

The Scenario & Constraint

The product requires sensitive data to remain protected and no such fallback is authorized. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Use only a supported bounded key-cache/workflow policy if its threat model permits; otherwise return controlled unavailability and recover the key dependency without writing unprotected data.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Availability suffers and caches extend key exposure. A plaintext fallback silently changes the protection promise rather than solving the key-service outage.

A restored backup needs a retired key. What should the lifecycle have included?

The Scenario & Constraint

The backup retention period exceeds the key material’s recoverable lifetime. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Recover the authorized historical key if possible, align key/version retention with data restore obligations and test complete backup restores before future retirement.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Longer key retention adds protection/audit burden. Destroying the only required key can make recovery impossible; a new unrelated key cannot decrypt old ciphertext.

A certificate change breaks clients. Disable hostname verification?

The Scenario & Constraint

The connection must authenticate the intended service. What will you do and why?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Repair certificate names/chains/trust distribution and rollout using the supported TLS stack, with explicit recovery for affected clients while keeping peer verification.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Correct rollout can take time. Turning verification off may preserve encrypted bytes to the wrong peer, which is not the required authenticated channel.
