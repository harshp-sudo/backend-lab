Master-Slave Architecture

🎯 The Hook & ELI5

A school office has one clerk authorized to change the official register and other clerks who read copies. If the official clerk is unavailable, appointing a replacement requires making sure the old clerk cannot keep issuing conflicting changes. A second desk alone does not establish who is in charge. This lesson examines that one-writer, copied-reader arrangement and the handover protocol around it.

The requested historical title is Master-Slave Architecture; current terminology is primary–replica, primary–standby or leader–follower. One logical primary accepts authoritative writes for the data boundary, and replicas copy its changes. Eligible reads may be routed to followers under a declared freshness contract.

The Replication lesson explains copying/acknowledgment guarantees. Here we focus on operating the topology: discovering the current writer, deciding when a standby may be promoted, preventing conflicting writers, reconnecting applications and restoring redundancy afterward. PostgreSQL 18 standby/failover documentation provides a concrete example; built-in coordination differs across database products.

🗺️ High-Level Big Picture

Replication, role and routing are separate mechanisms. A primary-role check is not sufficient proof of current write authority when an old isolated node can still believe it owns the role.

🔍 Step-by-Step Mechanics

Follow the worked example one decision at a time. Open each step to connect the everyday model to the implementation; SQL examples target PostgreSQL 18 unless explicitly labeled otherwise.

1. Assign one write authority for the data boundary

A single-primary design simplifies ordering and conflict ownership: all accepted writes for this boundary pass through one authority. This does not mean the entire company has one database writer; different shards or databases can have separate owners.

Define how authority is granted, renewed and revoked. A cluster manager, supported election system or another rigorously designed coordination mechanism owns that decision. Do not replace it with an arbitrary app-side preference for whichever host returns a TCP response fastest.

2. Give applications explicit read and write routes

Use stable connection discovery/endpoints appropriate to the platform, with checks for current authority and service readiness. Replicas that are alive but lagging or recovering may be unsuitable for a particular read. A read transaction requiring its own write must remain on an appropriate connection/boundary.

Document what happens after routing changes: cached addresses, old sockets and pool entries can outlive a DNS update. Make failures bounded and reconnect through verified discovery. A read-only role flag can prevent accidental writes but cannot establish that a writable old node still owns the current epoch.

3. Distinguish suspicion from a safe takeover

A missed heartbeat may mean the primary crashed, the detector is isolated, or the network is delayed. A correct failover protocol considers these possibilities and prevents simultaneous authoritative writers. Fencing removes an old owner’s ability to write or make its writes authoritative, using mechanisms appropriate to the deployment.

A supported quorum/lease/election design may help establish authority, but it must be implemented with its assumptions intact. A time-based guess or a witness added without a tested protocol is not automatic split-brain prevention. PostgreSQL does not itself supply the complete external failure-detection/orchestration system described here.

4. Promote according to data-loss and recovery rules

Check candidate health, durable/applied progress and the configured replication policy before promotion. Choose what loss is acceptable and how it is detected. Fence/retire old ownership and publish new ownership/routing through the supported sequence; the exact order and barriers depend on the cluster mechanism.

Measure recovery from the client’s perspective, including reconnects, retries, warmup and required read freshness. A command reporting “promoted” is only one milestone. A deliberately slower, safe handover may be preferable to quickly accepting divergent writes.

5. Handle in-flight writes as possibly unknown outcomes

A connection can disappear after commit but before the client receives success. Repeating a charge/order with a new operation identity may duplicate a committed action. Give retryable business operations a stable request identity and a durable result/semantic duplicate policy, using the authoritative database or operation owner.

Reconcile the existing outcome before applying again, and validate that the same identity carries the same intent. This is separate from a database transaction’s rollback behavior: neither a lost socket nor an attempted rollback proves what the server already committed.

6. Rejoin the old node and restore a protected topology

A returned old primary may have divergent history. Keep it fenced, compare its state using the engine’s documented procedure, and rebuild/rewind/reseed it as appropriate before admitting it as a replica. Never simply point old and new primaries at clients and hope replication merges them.

After promotion, redundancy can be degraded until another suitable standby exists. Verify backup chains, replication health and the new failure envelope. Practice planned switchovers, including application routing and a second failure during recovery, rather than treating failover as a one-time installation task.

⚠️ Common Pitfalls, Bugs & What-If Scenarios

Read each failure as a story: diagnose the visible symptom, identify its cause, and change the mechanism that produced it.

Timeout appoints a second writer

Symptom: Two hosts accept conflicting orders during a network split.

Root Cause: Failure suspicion was treated as proof the original owner was harmless.

Fix/Prevention: Use supported ownership coordination and fencing; test partitions as well as host crashes.

DNS change leaves stale sessions

Symptom: Some clients continue hitting the retired primary.

Root Cause: Existing connections/caches were assumed to follow new routing instantly.

Fix/Prevention: Fence the retired owner, bound/discard stale sessions and reconnect through verified ownership discovery.

Promotion ends the recovery work

Symptom: A second failure loses service because no standby is ready.

Root Cause: The newly promoted primary was never given a restored redundancy/backup plan.

Fix/Prevention: Track the degraded period, rejoin/rebuild replicas safely and verify the new topology’s failure envelope.

🧠 Active Recall & Mastery Check

Summarize this in your own words: A primary–replica topology needs one valid writer, honest read routing and a complete takeover/rejoin protocol. Promotion is one step; safe ownership and usable client recovery are the actual outcome. Explain a concrete example, name an assumption that changes the answer, and sketch the relevant diagram without looking.

Is “this server is writable” enough to identify the valid primary?

No. An old isolated node can retain its local role. Current write authority must be established by the deployment’s supported ownership/fencing mechanism.

What can a missed heartbeat prove?

Only that a response was not observed under the detector’s conditions. It does not alone distinguish a crash from delay or a network partition.

Why can a lost commit response require business-level idempotency?

The database may have committed even though the client saw a transport failure. A stable operation identity and recorded outcome allow a retry to return/reconcile the same intent.

Why not immediately return the old primary to traffic?

It may have divergent state or stale authority. Keep it fenced and use the documented repair/reseed/rejoin process before restoring its role in the new topology.

Tricky & Most-Asked Interview Scenarios

Both sides of a partition believe the primary failed. Who may accept writes?

The Scenario & Constraint

A primary and standby lose contact but each remains reachable by some clients. The standby’s watchdog wants to promote immediately. Conflicting order histories are unacceptable. What will you do?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Use the platform’s supported authority/election and fencing protocol so only a valid owner can accept authoritative writes. Do not promote solely because one heartbeat timed out. Keep an isolated old owner from serving writes, verify the candidate’s data eligibility, and make routing reflect the established ownership state. Test this partition explicitly before relying on automatic takeover.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Preserving one authority can make some requests fail or wait during uncertainty. A multi-writer/conflict-resolving model changes the data contract and is not a drop-in fix. A witness or lease is useful only within a correctly designed protocol with its timing/failure assumptions satisfied.

The new writer is healthy but half the app still uses old connections. How will recovery finish?

The Scenario & Constraint

A database endpoint changes after promotion. Long-lived connection pools retain sockets to the retired writer, and users see inconsistent failures. What will you change?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Ensure retired ownership is fenced independently of client cooperation. Make application/driver pools detect and discard stale/broken sessions, reconnect through current supported discovery, and bound acquisition/execution retries with jitter. Check readiness against the current ownership contract rather than connectivity alone. Measure recovery until real requests complete through the new owner.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Aggressive reconnects can create a connection storm, while waiting for every socket to expire extends the outage. A role/read-only probe helps routing but is not sufficient against an old writable split-brain node. Preserve operation identity when a request’s earlier commit outcome is uncertain.

The original host returns with writes absent from the new primary. Can it be reattached directly?

The Scenario & Constraint

An asynchronous failover promoted a standby while the old host was unreachable. The original host later restarts and contains a different history. What recovery path will you follow?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Keep the returned node fenced from authoritative clients. Determine the divergence and acknowledged-data-loss implications with the documented engine procedure, preserve required forensic data, and rebuild/rewind/reseed the node as appropriate for its prerequisites. Rejoin only after it follows the new authoritative history and passes health/replication checks.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Rebuilding costs time and bandwidth, but serving two histories can corrupt the business further. Automatic merging of arbitrary SQL histories is not a safe default. If some clients received success for lost writes, reconcile those operation identities under a defined recovery policy rather than silently discarding customer-visible outcomes.

A transaction starts on a replica and later needs to write. Can a router move it mid-transaction?

The Scenario & Constraint

An ORM lazily begins a transaction on a read endpoint, then attempts an update. The design assumes the update can simply be forwarded to the current primary. What will you do?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Route a transaction that can write to the appropriate authoritative connection from its beginning, or use a documented database-specific mechanism with its actual guarantees. Do not split a normal local transaction across unrelated sessions and claim it is still one atomic unit. Review ORM transaction classification and make read-only endpoints/features explicit.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Sending more transactions to the primary reduces read offload but preserves a clear boundary. Restarting a safe operation on the primary may be possible, with full transaction retry/idempotency rules. A follower snapshot and a primary update do not automatically form one consistent cross-session decision.

The connection fails just after COMMIT. Should checkout create a new order on retry?

The Scenario & Constraint

The primary may have committed an order before failover, but the client received no response. Retrying with a new order/request identifier could create a second purchase. What will you do?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Retry or query with the same stable business operation identity, using an authoritative durable result/deduplication policy that checks semantic equivalence. Reconcile whether the operation already took effect before creating a new one. Return an honest pending/unknown state when the outcome cannot yet be established; a network error does not prove rollback.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Keeping idempotency results requires retention and conflict rules, and a lost asynchronous write can complicate recovery of that record too. The policy must match the replication loss envelope. Blindly retrying every timeout or changing the identifier turns transport uncertainty into duplicated business intent.

A maintenance switchover promotes quickly but service takes minutes to recover. What was not tested?

The Scenario & Constraint

A team measures only the promotion command. During planned maintenance, stale routes, cold caches and pool retries extend the customer outage. How will you improve the procedure?

The Direct Answer & Architectural Decision

Define client-visible recovery criteria and rehearse the complete sequence: candidate catch-up, safe old-owner retirement, supported promotion, routing publication, controlled connection replacement, readiness checks and post-cutover data verification. Include warmup and load admission, then restore standby redundancy and validate backup/monitoring configuration before calling the operation complete.

Key Trade-Offs & Follow-Up Traps

Prewarming and rehearsals consume capacity and preparation time. Planned pauses can simplify a handover when allowed, but they do not excuse untested data-loss assumptions. A second failure during the degraded period needs its own recovery expectation rather than being omitted from the service objective.
